Pinocchio left the Tunny and followed the faint light through darkness.
Cold water splashed beneath his feet with every uncertain step.
The Shark’s deep breathing rolled around him like distant thunder.
At first, the golden point seemed no closer than a star.
Still, Pinocchio kept walking because waiting offered no rescue.
The light slowly became a candle fixed inside a glass bottle.
It stood on a small table surrounded by boxes and barrels.
Near the flame sat an old man with snowy hair.
He wore torn clothes and hungrily ate a wriggling fish.
The sight was strange, but the bent shoulders looked familiar.
Pinocchio stepped nearer and studied the tired face in silence.
The old man raised his eyes towards the unexpected visitor.
In the candlelight, Pinocchio recognised the father he had lost.
Joy struck him so strongly that his wooden knees nearly failed.
“Father, I have found you at last!” he cried.
Geppetto rubbed his eyes, afraid that hope was deceiving him.
Then Pinocchio threw both arms around his father’s thin neck.
They laughed, wept, and held each other beneath the flickering light.
For a while, neither of them could form a complete sentence.
Geppetto touched Pinocchio’s face as if testing a wonderful dream.
Pinocchio promised that he would never willingly leave him again.
“Are you truly my own boy?” Geppetto asked at last.
“Yes, and I have searched everywhere for you,” Pinocchio answered.
The candle shook in its bottle as the Shark moved around them.
Even inside the monster, their small corner suddenly felt like home.
Yet Geppetto needed to know how his son had reached this place.
Pinocchio began with the schoolbook bought using Geppetto’s only coat.
He admitted selling it to enter the Great Marionette Theatre.
He described Fire-Eater, the five gold pieces, and his hopeful journey.
Then came the Fox, the Cat, and their Field of Wonders.
He confessed each lie that had stretched his famous wooden nose.
He told how robbers chased him and left him hanging from a tree.
The Blue Fairy had rescued him, forgiven him, and offered guidance.
Still, he had chased shortcuts, trusted flatterers, and broken promises.
Pinocchio spoke of prison, the watchdog’s collar, and the green fisherman.
He remembered school fights, the sea, and his search for Geppetto.
He described the busy island where honest work could build a life.
Then he confessed abandoning that chance for Lamp-Wick and Toyland.
“Every mistake began when I chose excitement over love,” he finished.
Geppetto listened without interrupting, anger, pity, and relief crossing his face.
When the confession ended, he drew Pinocchio close once more.
Now it was Geppetto’s turn to explain his long disappearance.
He had built a tiny boat and sailed out searching for Pinocchio.
A sudden storm had swept the little craft far from shore.
Waves broke the boat, and the Terrible Shark swallowed Geppetto.
At first, he expected his life to end in darkness.
Then the monster swallowed a merchant ship loaded with provisions.
The wreck brought biscuits, preserved food, candles, and dry matches.
Those supplies had kept Geppetto alive for nearly two years.
He had counted days by the candles and changes in his beard.
Now the final scraps of food lay beside the final flame.
When that candle died, both warmth and direction would disappear.
“Then we must leave before it goes out,” Pinocchio declared.
Geppetto looked at his weak legs and shook his head.
“I cannot walk far, and I have never learnt to swim.”
Pinocchio answered before fear could settle between them.
“You carried me through life; tonight, I shall carry you.”
The words surprised Geppetto more than any tale of magic.
This was not the reckless puppet who had once fled home.
Pinocchio searched the wreckage for a safe path through the monster.
Geppetto explained that the ancient Shark suffered badly from asthma.
Each night, it slept with its enormous mouth partly open.
If they moved quietly, the open mouth might become their doorway.
The candle was already shorter, so they could not delay.
Geppetto took the bottle, and Pinocchio offered his steady hand.
Together they climbed through the Shark’s vast, slippery throat.
The walls rose and fell as the sleeping creature breathed.
They placed each foot carefully, afraid to wake their prison.
At last, cool night air touched Pinocchio’s wooden face.
Moonlight entered through the open jaws and silvered the tongue.
The tongue stretched before them like a wide country road.
Beyond three rows of teeth, the quiet sea promised freedom.
Geppetto stared at the drop and tightened his grip.
“Stay close,” Pinocchio whispered, leading him towards the edge.
They were preparing to jump when the Shark’s breathing suddenly changed.
Its whole body tightened around a powerful, rumbling breath.
The Shark sneezed with the force of a bursting cannon.
Wind swept Pinocchio and Geppetto backwards across the enormous tongue.
They tumbled down the throat and crashed beside the wreckage again.
The candle struck the water, hissed once, and went dark.
Geppetto’s voice shook when he whispered, “Now we are lost.”
Pinocchio felt fear too, but he did not let it lead.
“We are together, and we still know the way,” he replied.
He found Geppetto’s hand and helped him stand in the blackness.
Without the flame, every step depended on memory and trust.
They climbed the throat again while the Shark settled back to sleep.
This time, Pinocchio waited until each breath became slow and even.
They crossed the tongue and passed all three rows of teeth.
At the final edge, Geppetto could see only moonlit water below.
Pinocchio bent down and told his father to climb aboard.
Geppetto wrapped both arms tightly around the puppet’s neck.
“Do not be afraid,” Pinocchio said, though his own heart raced.
He leapt beyond the teeth and plunged into the cold sea.
For one terrible second, the weight of Geppetto pulled him down.
Then his wooden body rose, and his arms found a swimming rhythm.
Behind them, the Shark continued sleeping beneath the peaceful moon.
Ahead, no shore was visible across the dark water.
Pinocchio adjusted his father’s weight and chose a direction by the stars.
Geppetto rested against his back, too exhausted to speak.
Each stroke carried them farther from the mouth that had imprisoned them.
The journey remained dangerous, but father and son faced it together.
Pinocchio had entered the Shark searching for escape and found his purpose.
Now the boy made of wood was carrying his maker home.
