Pinocchio swam through the night with Geppetto on his back.
He said that land was not far away.
However, he could not see it clearly.
Geppetto shook from cold, fear, and hunger.
Soon Pinocchio’s wooden arms became very weak.
“Help me, Father,” he whispered in the water.
A friendly voice answered from somewhere nearby.
The Tunny had followed them out of the Shark.
He lifted both travellers onto his broad back.
Then he carried them safely towards the shore.
At sunrise, Pinocchio helped Geppetto onto the sand.
He kissed the kind Tunny and thanked him.
Father and son walked away to find a home.
Two poor animals were begging beside the road.
They were the Fox and Cat from long ago.
The Cat was blind, and the Fox had no tail.
Their lies had left them hungry and alone.
They called Pinocchio their friend and asked for money.
Their voices sounded kind, but he remembered everything.
They had stolen his coins with false promises.
“You tricked me once, but not again,” he said.
The Fox gave excuses, and the Cat copied him.
Pinocchio did not shout or laugh at them.
He took Geppetto’s arm and walked away.
Now he could see danger inside sweet words.
He no longer wanted an easy road to riches.
Soon they saw a tiny cottage made of straw.
A hidden voice told them to open the door.
They entered, but the little room looked empty.
Then they saw the Talking Cricket above them.
The Cricket remembered Pinocchio’s terrible hammer throw.
Pinocchio did not try to defend himself.
He lowered his head and said he was sorry.
“Please help my father, even if you punish me.”
The Cricket believed this honest and simple apology.
He welcomed them into the cottage to stay.
The Blue Fairy had given him the little house.
Pinocchio cried because she thought he was dead.
Then he made a soft straw bed for Geppetto.
Geppetto needed milk, but they had no money.
The Cricket sent Pinocchio to Farmer John.
The farmer offered milk in return for work.
Pinocchio had to pull up one hundred water buckets.
The rough rope hurt his hands badly.
Each full bucket felt heavier than the last.
Pinocchio wanted to stop many times.
Then he remembered his weak father at home.
He finally poured the last bucket into the trough.
Farmer John gave him warm and sweet milk.
Pinocchio carried every drop home to Geppetto.
The next morning, he did the work again.
Daily work slowly made his father stronger.
At the farm, Pinocchio saw a sick donkey.
The animal lay quietly on some dirty straw.
Its tired eyes seemed very familiar to him.
Pinocchio bent down and asked its name.
“I am Lamp-Wick,” the donkey answered weakly.
Pinocchio knew his old friend at once.
Toyland had led Lamp-Wick to this hard life.
The poor donkey died before help could come.
The farmer only saw an animal without value.
Pinocchio saw a friend and a painful warning.
He returned home and told Geppetto the sad news.
The memory helped him make better choices.
Pinocchio returned to the well every morning.
He continued this hard work for five months.
He also learnt to make baskets from reeds.
He sold the baskets and bought food.
Then he built a rolling chair for Geppetto.
They went outside together on warm, bright days.
Every night, Pinocchio studied beside a lamp.
He bought an old book with missing pages.
He made ink from dark berries.
He wrote words with a sharp wooden stick.
Learning was slow, but Pinocchio kept trying.
His father grew healthy, and their home felt peaceful.
After paying their costs, Pinocchio saved fifty pennies.
He wanted a new coat, hat, and shoes.
This time, he had truly earned the reward.
On his way to market, he met the Snail.
She used to live with the Blue Fairy.
The Snail said that the Fairy was very ill.
She had no money to buy food.
Pinocchio gave the Snail all fifty pennies.
“My old clothes can wait,” he said.
He promised to work harder and send more.
At home, Geppetto asked about the new clothes.
Pinocchio said that he would shop another day.
That night, he made sixteen baskets instead of eight.
He worked quietly until the clock reached midnight.
Then he slept beside his dear father.
The Blue Fairy came to him in a dream.
She looked healthy, warm, and very happy.
She praised his loving and helpful choices.
“You helped others when it was difficult,” she said.
The Fairy forgave his old mistakes and kissed him.
Morning sunlight filled a beautiful new room.
Pinocchio looked down at two warm hands.
His wooden body had become a living boy.
He saw bright eyes and brown hair in the mirror.
New clothes and fifty gold coins waited nearby.
Healthy Geppetto was working in the next room.
Pinocchio ran to him with happy tears.
Geppetto said Pinocchio’s choices had changed their home.
The old wooden puppet rested beside a chair.
Pinocchio looked at it without feeling ashamed.
Magic had changed his body during one night.
Love and hard work had changed his heart first.
At last, the real boy and his father were home.
