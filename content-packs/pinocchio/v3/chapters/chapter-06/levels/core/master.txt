When the prison gate opened, Pinocchio hurried towards the Fairy’s house.
He felt ashamed of every foolish promise he had broken.
This time, he meant to apologise without another excuse.
The road crossed a lonely field beneath the afternoon sun.
A gigantic green Serpent stretched from hedge to hedge.
Smoke curled from its nostrils, and fire flickered in its eyes.
Pinocchio stopped so sharply that his wooden heels struck together.
“Excuse me, could you move your tail?” he asked politely.
The Serpent did not answer or even blink.
Pinocchio tried to step over it, but the great tail rose.
He sprang backwards, tripped, and landed headfirst in the mud.
The sight was so ridiculous that the Serpent shook with laughter.
It laughed until it collapsed across the road, leaving Pinocchio free to pass.
Pinocchio cleaned his face, but evening approached.
He had eaten nothing since leaving prison, and hunger sharpened every smell.
Beyond a wall, purple grapes hung from the vines.
“I shall take only one bunch,” he told himself.
He climbed over the wall and reached between the leaves.
At once, an iron trap snapped around his wooden ankles.
Pinocchio pulled, twisted, and begged for release.
The harder he struggled, the deeper its teeth gripped.
Cold rain fell, soaking the leaves and darkening the soil.
Pinocchio remembered the Woodpecker, the Cat, and every ignored warning.
“Thieves always find a reason,” he whispered, “and I have found mine.”
No one heard his confession except the crickets in the vineyard.
He remained trapped until a farmer arrived carrying a lantern.
The farmer set the trap because thieves stole his chickens.
When he saw Pinocchio, he frowned at the ruined vines.
“A wooden thief is still a thief,” the farmer declared.
Pinocchio explained that hunger had tempted him towards the grapes.
The truth did not erase what he had chosen to do.
Instead of taking him to prison, the farmer chose another punishment.
He dragged Pinocchio to the farmyard beside the chicken coop.
There stood an empty kennel and a chain with a heavy collar.
The farmer’s watchdog, Melampo, had died days before.
“Tonight, you will guard my chickens in his place,” he said.
He fastened the collar around Pinocchio’s neck and locked the chain.
Pinocchio crouched beside the kennel, humiliated and unable to escape.
For the first time, freedom seemed less valuable than a clean conscience.
Near midnight, four lean Weasels slipped through the hedge.
They were surprised to find Pinocchio where Melampo had once slept.
Their leader approached and spoke as if discussing ordinary business.
Every week, the Weasels had taken eight chickens from the coop.
They kept seven and secretly gave Melampo one for staying silent.
“Accept the same arrangement,” she whispered, “and everyone will be satisfied.”
Pinocchio thought of the grapes and the collar around his neck.
One dishonest bargain had already brought him enough shame.
Still, he nodded and pretended to accept their offer.
The Weasels crept inside while Pinocchio waited beside the door.
Then he slammed the coop shut and dropped the wooden bar.
He barked until lights appeared in the farmhouse windows.
The farmer rushed outside with his lantern and found the thieves trapped.
He asked Pinocchio how he had discovered their secret arrangement.
Pinocchio could have blamed Melampo and made himself look clever.
Instead, he repeated every word of the Weasels’ offer.
He also admitted that he had pretended to join them.
The farmer listened carefully, then removed the heavy collar.
“You stole my grapes, but tonight you protected what was mine.”
Pinocchio rubbed his neck and promised never to steal food again.
“Remember the promise when you are hungry,” the farmer replied.
He opened the gate and allowed the puppet to leave at sunrise.
Pinocchio walked away without reward, yet his steps felt lighter.
Honesty had not cancelled his mistake, but it had changed his next choice.
By midday, Pinocchio reached the place where the Fairy’s cottage had stood.
The bright house, the garden, and the welcoming door had vanished.
Only a small marble stone remained among the wild grass.
Words carved upon it said the Blue Fairy had died of sorrow.
They claimed that Pinocchio’s desertion had broken her heart.
He fell to his knees and wrapped both arms around the stone.
“Please come back,” he cried, “and I will finally deserve your kindness.”
No answer came from the empty field.
Pinocchio wept until his voice became a dry wooden whisper.
He remembered her medicine, her patience, and her impossible forgiveness.
Now every second chance seemed precious because he believed they were gone.
The sky darkened while he remained beside the silent stone.
For once, he did not run from the pain he had caused.
He stayed there, grieving, until wings beat powerfully above him.
A Pigeon circled the field and landed beside the stone.
The bird had known Geppetto and recognised the puppet immediately.
“Your father has searched everywhere for you,” the Pigeon said.
For months, Geppetto had crossed towns, forests, and distant roads.
At last, he had built a tiny boat beside the coast.
He planned to sail across the ocean and search foreign countries.
Pinocchio sprang up, suddenly filled with hope and fear.
The coast lay more than a thousand miles away.
“Climb onto my back,” said the Pigeon, lowering one broad wing.
Pinocchio held tightly as they rose above roofs and fields.
They flew through cold clouds, rain, and a moonless night.
By morning, the smell of salt reached them from the sea.
The Pigeon landed near a beach crowded with anxious people.
Far offshore, a tiny boat climbed and disappeared between enormous waves.
Pinocchio narrowed his eyes and recognised Geppetto at the oars.
“Father!” he shouted, though the storm tore the word apart.
Geppetto saw a small figure on the shore and waved his cap.
Before he could turn the boat, a dark wall of water struck it.
For one terrible moment, both the boat and Geppetto vanished.
The crowd begged Pinocchio not to enter the violent sea.
He remembered every road his father had travelled to find him.
Then he threw off his cap and ran straight into the surf.
Wood floated well, but courage was carrying him farther than buoyancy.
He swam beyond the breakers, calling Geppetto’s name between waves.
Night fell, and the coast became a thin line behind him.
Still, Pinocchio kicked towards the darkness where the boat had disappeared.
He could not know what waited beyond the storm.
He knew only that, this time, he would not abandon his father.
