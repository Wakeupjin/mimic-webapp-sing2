Released at last, Pinocchio hurried towards the Fairy’s home with urgent, guilty purpose.
Every broken promise travelled beside him, louder than his footsteps on the road.
This time, he intended to apologise without decorating failure as misfortune.
The road crossed a lonely field, pale beneath the descending afternoon sun.
Across it lay a colossal green Serpent, blocking the way from hedge to hedge.
Smoke coiled from its nostrils while sparks trembled behind its watchful eyes.
Pinocchio stopped so suddenly that his wooden heels struck like castanets.
“Would you kindly move your tail?” he asked with unusual politeness.
The Serpent offered no answer, no blink, and not the smallest movement.
Gathering courage, Pinocchio attempted to climb over its enormous body.
The tail rose without warning, sending him backwards into a pool of mud.
His muddy, upside-down appearance delighted the Serpent beyond all reasonable measure.
It laughed until it collapsed across the road, accidentally leaving space for escape.
Pinocchio wiped his face and continued as the light began fading.
Prison had provided no supper, so every evening scent sharpened his hunger.
Behind a vineyard wall, ripe purple grapes hung invitingly from the vines.
“One bunch is need, not theft,” Pinocchio argued with himself.
He climbed the wall and reached towards the nearest heavy cluster.
An iron trap snapped shut, fastening itself around both wooden ankles.
Pinocchio twisted and pulled, but the cruel mechanism refused every appeal.
The harder he struggled, the more deeply its metal teeth held him.
Cold rain swept through the vineyard, turning dust into dark, sticky earth.
Pinocchio recalled the Woodpecker, the Cat, and a parade of ignored warnings.
“A thief always finds a reason,” he whispered, “and now I have mine.”
Only the vineyard crickets remained to witness this uncomfortable piece of honesty.
He waited there until a farmer appeared behind the circle of lamplight.
The farmer had set the trap after thieves repeatedly raided his chicken coop.
Finding Pinocchio among damaged vines, he treated hunger as explanation, not excuse.
“Wooden or flesh, a thief remains a thief,” the farmer declared.
Pinocchio admitted that hunger had drawn him over the forbidden wall.
Speaking truthfully could not reverse the choice already made.
Rather than return him to prison, the farmer designed a more fitting punishment.
He marched Pinocchio across the farmyard towards the guarded chicken coop.
Beside it stood an empty kennel, a thick chain, and a humiliating collar.
Melampo, the farmer’s faithful watchdog, had died only a few days earlier.
“Tonight, you will protect my chickens in his place,” the farmer announced.
The collar closed around Pinocchio’s neck, and the chain fixed him to the kennel.
Unable to run, he crouched in the straw and studied his fresh disgrace.
For once, freedom seemed less valuable than becoming someone who deserved it.
Near midnight, four lean Weasels slipped through a narrow opening in the hedge.
They stopped when Pinocchio appeared where their old partner Melampo had slept.
Their leader approached calmly, speaking with the manners of an established merchant.
Every week, the Weasels had removed eight chickens from the coop.
Seven became their dinner, while one purchased Melampo’s useful silence.
“Continue the arrangement,” she murmured, “and everyone receives something.”
Pinocchio felt the collar press against the memory of the stolen grapes.
Another dishonest bargain promised comfort, but now he recognised its actual price.
He nodded nevertheless, disguising refusal as eager agreement.
The Weasels entered the coop while Pinocchio waited beside the open door.
Then he slammed it shut and dropped the wooden bar into place.
His loud barking pulled lamplight rapidly across the farmhouse windows.
The farmer rushed outside and discovered four astonished thieves behind the door.
He asked how Pinocchio had uncovered their long-running arrangement.
Pinocchio might have blamed Melampo and presented himself as naturally clever.
Instead, he repeated the Weasels’ proposal without improving his own part.
He even admitted that deception had helped him capture them.
After listening carefully, the farmer unlocked the collar and released the chain.
“Yesterday you stole my grapes; tonight you protected what belonged to me.”
Pinocchio rubbed his neck and promised never again to steal his food.
“Remember that promise when hunger returns,” the farmer warned him.
At sunrise, the gate opened, offering freedom without reward or applause.
Pinocchio departed empty-handed, though his wooden steps felt unexpectedly light.
Honesty had not repaired yesterday, but it had redirected tomorrow.
By noon, he reached the field where the Fairy’s bright cottage had stood.
The house, garden, and welcoming doorway had vanished as completely as a dream.
Among the wild grass remained only a small stone of pale marble.
Its carved words announced that the Blue Fairy had died from sorrow.
The message blamed Pinocchio’s desertion for breaking her generous heart.
He dropped beside the stone and held it as though warmth might return.
“Come back,” he pleaded, “and I will finally become worthy of you.”
The empty field answered with wind moving through the grass.
Pinocchio wept until his voice sounded thin, dry, and wooden.
He remembered medicine, patience, laughter, and forgiveness he had treated as endless.
Now every second chance seemed priceless because he believed none remained.
Evening darkened around him while he stayed beside the silent memorial.
For once, Pinocchio neither fled his pain nor blamed another traveller.
He remained with it until powerful wings disturbed the air overhead.
A great Pigeon circled once, then landed beside the marble stone.
The bird had known Geppetto and immediately recognised his missing son.
“Your father has searched every road for you,” the Pigeon explained.
For months, Geppetto had crossed towns, forests, and unfamiliar country.
Finally, he built a fragile boat on the distant coast.
He meant to cross the ocean and continue searching among foreign lands.
Hope lifted Pinocchio to his feet, although fear rose beside it.
The coast waited more than a thousand miles from that lonely field.
“Take my back,” offered the Pigeon, lowering one strong wing.
Pinocchio held tightly as rooftops, roads, and farms shrank beneath them.
They crossed rain, freezing clouds, and the darkness of a moonless night.
By morning, salt entered the wind and the sea appeared ahead.
The Pigeon descended beside a beach crowded with anxious onlookers.
Far offshore, one tiny boat climbed and vanished between mountainous waves.
Pinocchio narrowed his eyes and recognised Geppetto struggling at the oars.
“Father!” he shouted, but the storm tore the name into pieces.
Geppetto noticed the figure on shore and raised his cap in answer.
Before he could turn, a wall of black water struck the boat broadside.
For one terrible moment, the wave erased both father and vessel.
People seized at Pinocchio, begging him not to challenge the violent sea.
He remembered every mile Geppetto had travelled while searching for him.
Then Pinocchio dropped his cap and ran directly into the breaking surf.
Wood provided buoyancy, but courage carried him beyond the safer water.
He swam through the breakers, calling Geppetto’s name whenever he could breathe.
Night gathered, and the crowded beach faded into a distant grey line.
Still, Pinocchio kicked towards the darkness that had swallowed the boat.
He could not know whether danger, rescue, or grief waited beyond the storm.
He knew only that he would not abandon his father a second time.
