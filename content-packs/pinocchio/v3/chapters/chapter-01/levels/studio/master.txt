Long ago, in a quiet Tuscan town, Master Cherry worked alone among curls of fragrant wood.
His neighbours had given him that name because excitement turned his round nose cherry-red.
One bitter winter morning, he noticed an ordinary block beside his crowded bench.
Nothing about the timber suggested beauty, rarity, or hidden worth.
Even so, its solid shape promised one perfectly useful table leg.
Master Cherry warmed his hands, raised the axe, and murmured, “You will do splendidly.”
Before steel met timber, a frightened little voice pleaded, “Please, do not strike me!”
The carpenter stopped so abruptly that the axe remained suspended above him.
He searched under the bench, behind the door, and through every dusty cupboard.
The workshop held only tools, shavings, and a silence that felt newly suspicious.
“My imagination is playing tricks,” he concluded, although his knees disagreed.
Drawing a careful breath, he lifted the heavy axe once more.
The blade landed, and the wounded voice protested, “Oh! That truly hurt!”
Master Cherry released the axe and regarded the block with genuine alarm.
Ordinary wood had no voice, yet this piece plainly possessed strong opinions.
Hoping for a sensible explanation, he ran a plane across its rough surface.
As the tool scraped forwards, the timber giggled with the delight of a naughty child.
Master Cherry lurched backwards and disappeared into a basket overflowing with shavings.
Colour drained from his face, returned violently, and vanished again.
“Somebody must be deceiving me,” he muttered, more hopefully than confidently.
He approached the block and touched it with one cautious finger.
The wood offered no answer.
Encouraged, he pressed rather harder.
A deep, injured groan emerged from somewhere inside the grain.
At precisely that moment, a forceful knock rattled the workshop door.
Master Cherry nearly leapt clean out of his leather apron.
He shoved the impossible block aside and called, rather too loudly, “Come in!”
Geppetto entered in a mended coat, carrying poverty lightly and hope openly.
His purse was nearly empty, but his imagination contained entire theatres.
“My dear friend, I have conceived a remarkable plan,” he announced.
“I shall build a wooden boy who dances, fences, and performs perfect somersaults.”
Master Cherry studied him, uncertain whether admiration or concern was required.
The puppet might travel from town to town and earn their daily bread.
Beneath that practical scheme lay Geppetto’s deeper wish for companionship.
His single room felt painfully quiet when evening settled over it.
“All I require is wood with a little spirit,” he explained.
From the neglected corner came an unmistakably amused snort.
Master Cherry glanced towards the troublesome block and quickly made a decision.
Perhaps Geppetto’s lively dream deserved this equally lively piece of timber.
“Take the block near the wall,” Master Cherry offered with suspicious generosity.
Geppetto bent towards it, already imagining the figure hidden inside.
Before his fingers arrived, the secret voice chirped, “Good afternoon, Polendina!”
The nickname mocked Geppetto’s corn-yellow wig and wounded his considerable pride.
He swung around, cheeks blazing.
“Which one of us said that?”
Master Cherry raised both hands and denied the insult with complete sincerity.
Neither proud old man found the other’s innocence convincing.
Accusations gathered force until words, stamping boots, and waving arms became a storm.
Within moments, they rolled across the floor like furious sacks of flour.
Eventually they stopped, breathless, dusty, and rather embarrassed by themselves.
Geppetto carefully straightened Master Cherry’s twisted collar.
Master Cherry brushed a cloud of shavings from Geppetto’s patched shoulders.
“Shall we be friends again?” Geppetto asked, still panting.
“Friends again,” Master Cherry agreed.
From the corner, the block rewarded its performance with a satisfied chuckle.
Master Cherry wrapped it immediately and pressed it into Geppetto’s waiting arms.
As Geppetto crossed the narrow streets, he pictured his puppet commanding a delighted audience.
There would be music, applause, travel, and perhaps regular meals beside the fire.
Reality offered one cramped room tucked beneath a steep staircase.
Its painted fireplace supplied cheerful colour but absolutely no warmth.
Geppetto placed the wood upon his bench and lit his smallest lamp.
“Every proper boy needs a proper name,” he reflected.
After considering several grand possibilities, his expression brightened.
“You shall be called Pinocchio.”
The name sounded nimble, bright, and ready to tumble into adventure.
Geppetto sharpened each tool, treating the strange timber with patient respect.
“Let us carve a boy,” he whispered, “and perhaps discover a family.”
Under the warm lamplight, he shaped soft hair, a forehead, and two alert eyes.
The instant those eyes appeared, they fixed themselves boldly upon their maker.
“A son might look at his father kindly,” Geppetto suggested.
The newly carved eyes rolled towards the ceiling in theatrical refusal.
Next came the nose, which lengthened whenever Geppetto attempted to correct it.
He trimmed it repeatedly, but the stubborn feature always reclaimed its size.
With a weary sigh, Geppetto turned his attention to the mouth.
No sooner had he shaped the lips than they curled into a wicked grin.
Then the unfinished face laughed straight at him.
“You possess no body, yet you have already discovered bad manners.”
The grin broadened, entirely untroubled by the criticism.
Geppetto carved the chin, neck, shoulders, arms, and restless hands.
The moment those hands were free, they snatched his yellow wig.
Pinocchio waved the stolen wig like a victory flag and laughed.
“Return that at once,” Geppetto ordered, though hurt now outweighed anger.
He completed the body, legs, and feet while quietly wiping away one tear.
When Geppetto set him upright, Pinocchio kicked his nose and twisted free.
One uncertain step became two, then exploded into a reckless circuit of the room.
Pinocchio overturned a stool, sprang across the bed, and charged towards freedom.
“Stop this instant!” Geppetto shouted.
The puppet lifted the latch and shot into the street without hesitation.
He darted between market stalls, scattering chickens and delighting several children.
Geppetto pursued him in thin slippers, calling his new son’s name.
Pinocchio never turned around.
Freedom dazzled him, while consequences remained too distant to imagine.
A constable heard the uproar and calmly occupied the centre of the road.
Pinocchio attempted to pass, but the officer captured him by his extraordinary nose.
His legs continued cycling uselessly above the cobbles.
Geppetto arrived gasping, equal parts exhausted, frightened, and relieved.
“Thank goodness you have stopped him,” he said.
“He is my boy, and I mean to bring him safely home.”
Geppetto reached for Pinocchio’s ears and discovered he had carved none.
When he caught the puppet’s neck instead, Pinocchio collapsed dramatically upon the road.
The gathering crowd mistook resistance for mistreatment and judged Geppetto without evidence.
Rather than examine the scene, the constable obeyed the noisiest accusations.
He freed the puppet and took the bewildered carpenter firmly by the arm.
As evening darkened, Pinocchio escaped while his innocent father was marched to prison.
“Oh, Pinocchio,” Geppetto sighed, “I carved you with love but neglected wisdom.”
Somewhere ahead, the first consequence of Pinocchio’s freedom was already waiting.
