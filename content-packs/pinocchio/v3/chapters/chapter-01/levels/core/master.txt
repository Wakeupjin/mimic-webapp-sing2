Long ago, in a quiet Tuscan town, an old carpenter named Master Cherry worked alone in his dusty shop.
Everyone called him Master Cherry because the end of his nose was round and red, especially when he became excited.
One winter morning, he discovered a plain block of wood beside his bench.
It did not look rare or valuable.
Still, it seemed perfect for a table leg.
Master Cherry rubbed his hands, lifted his axe, and whispered, “This will do nicely.”
Before the blade touched the wood, a tiny voice cried, “Please don’t hit me!”
The carpenter froze.
He looked under the bench, behind the door, near the stove, and even inside an empty cupboard.
No one was there.
“I must have imagined it,” he decided, though his knees had begun to shake.
He raised the axe again.
This time, the voice squealed, “That tickles!”
Master Cherry dropped the axe and stared at the block.
A piece of wood could not speak, yet this one clearly had opinions.
He picked up a plane and tried to smooth its rough surface with three careful strokes.
The moment the tool scraped across it, the wood laughed like a cheeky child.
Master Cherry stumbled backwards and landed in a basket of shavings.
His face went pale, then red, then pale again.
“There must be a trick,” he muttered.
He tapped the block gently with one finger.
The wood remained silent.
He tapped it a little harder.
It gave a long, offended groan.
At that exact moment, someone knocked loudly at the shop door.
Master Cherry nearly jumped out of his apron.
He pushed the strange block into a corner and called, “Come in!”
Geppetto entered, carrying a patched coat and a hopeful smile.
He was a poor woodcarver, but his imagination was richer than the treasure room of any king.
“I have a wonderful plan,” he announced.
“I shall make a wooden boy who can dance, fence, and turn somersaults.”
Master Cherry blinked.
Geppetto explained that the puppet could travel with him and earn enough money for bread and a glass of wine.
More than money, however, Geppetto wanted company in his small, lonely house at the end of each day.
“I only need a piece of wood with a little character,” he said.
From the corner came a soft snort.
Master Cherry glanced nervously at the block.
Perhaps, he thought, this troublesome wood belonged with Geppetto.
“Take that piece over there,” he offered.
Geppetto bent down to examine the block.
Before he touched it, the hidden voice called, “Good afternoon, Polendina!”
Polendina was a rude nickname for Geppetto’s yellow wig.
Geppetto spun round, furious.
“Who said that?”
Master Cherry lifted both hands and swore it was not him.
Neither man believed the other.
Their argument became a storm of accusations, stamping feet, and flying insults.
A moment later, they were rolling across the floor like two angry sacks of flour.
At last, breathless and embarrassed, they stopped.
Geppetto straightened Master Cherry’s collar.
Master Cherry dusted Geppetto’s coat.
“Friends again?” Geppetto asked.
“Friends again,” Master Cherry replied.
The block gave a quiet, satisfied chuckle.
Master Cherry quickly wrapped the block and placed it in Geppetto’s arms.
As Geppetto walked home through the narrow streets, he imagined the puppet bowing before a delighted crowd.
He imagined music, applause, and warm meals shared beside the fire.
His real home was only one small room beneath a staircase.
A painted fire on the wall had to pretend to keep him warm.
Geppetto set the wood on his workbench and lit a little lamp.
“What shall I call you?” he wondered.
After a moment, he smiled.
“I shall call you Pinocchio.”
The name sounded bright, quick, and full of adventure.
Geppetto sharpened his tools with great care.
“Let us make a boy,” he said, “and perhaps a family too.”
First, under the warm circle of lamplight, Geppetto carved the hair, then the forehead, and then two lively eyes.
The moment the eyes appeared, they stared directly at him.
“It is polite to look kindly at your father,” Geppetto said.
The eyes rolled towards the ceiling.
Next, he carved a nose.
It grew longer and longer, no matter how often he shortened it.
Geppetto sighed and continued.
He shaped a mouth, and the mouth immediately began to grin.
Then it laughed at him.
“You have not even got a body, and already you are rude.”
The grin only widened.
Geppetto carved the chin, neck, shoulders, arms, and hands.
As soon as the hands were finished, they snatched away his yellow wig.
Pinocchio waved it like a flag and laughed.
“Give that back,” Geppetto ordered, trying not to lose his temper.
Pinocchio dropped the wig, but only because his new feet were ready.
Geppetto placed the puppet on the floor and held both hands to teach him to walk.
One step became two, and two steps became a wild dash around the room.
Pinocchio kicked over a stool, leapt across the bed, and raced towards the door.
“Stop!” cried Geppetto.
The puppet pulled the latch and shot into the street.
He ran between market stalls, startled chickens, and laughing children.
Geppetto hurried after him in his slippers, calling his name.
Pinocchio did not look back.
Freedom felt too exciting, and consequences were still a mystery.
A police constable heard the commotion and stood in the middle of the road.
Pinocchio tried to slip past, but the constable caught him by his remarkable nose.
His wooden legs continued running in the air.
Geppetto arrived, breathless and relieved.
“Thank goodness,” he said.
“That is my boy.”
The watching crowd began to whisper.
Some people decided that Geppetto must be a cruel father, although none of them knew the truth.
The constable listened to the loudest voices instead of the wisest one.
He released Pinocchio and took Geppetto by the arm.
Pinocchio raced away without understanding the trouble he had caused.
Geppetto was led to prison as evening settled over the town.
“Oh, Pinocchio,” he sighed, “I made you with love, but I forgot to give you sense.”
And somewhere ahead, the wooden boy’s first hard lesson was already waiting.
