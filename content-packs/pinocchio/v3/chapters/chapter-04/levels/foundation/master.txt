Fire Eater opened his bag and counted five gold coins.
“Take this money to your father,” he told Pinocchio.
The coins could buy food, wood, books, and a coat.
Pinocchio thanked him, and Fire Eater sneezed.
At sunrise, Pinocchio left the theatre for home.
He held one hand over the coins.
He imagined Geppetto’s happy and surprised face.
Each step made five small promises ring.
He would buy another book and fill the cupboard.
Nothing would stop him on the way home.
Then two travellers spoke to him in the square.
One was a Fox, and one was a Cat.
The Fox walked badly, and the Cat acted blind.
They smiled in a friendly but strange way.
The Fox asked why Pinocchio looked so happy.
Pinocchio proudly showed them his five gold coins.
The Cat suddenly saw the gold very clearly.
The Fox told him about the Field of Wonders.
In that field, a planted coin became a tree.
By morning, the tree would grow many gold coins.
Pinocchio imagined a warm new home for Geppetto.
The Fox sounded helpful and wise.
“Why work slowly when gold can grow?” he asked.
A Blackbird called to Pinocchio from a branch.
“Do not believe easy miracles from strangers,” it warned.
The Cat jumped up, and the bird flew away.
The Fox said that some birds trusted nobody.
Pinocchio felt worried, but he wanted the gold.
He agreed to walk with his new friends.
They said the field was almost on his way.
At night, they reached the Red Lobster Inn.
The Fox said good investors needed a large dinner.
He ordered meat, fish, bread, and sweet food.
The Cat ate a lot while still acting blind.
Pinocchio ate only nuts to save money.
The Fox and Cat praised his careful choice.
Then they all went to sleep until midnight.
Pinocchio put the coins safely under his pillow.
He dreamed about a tree full of gold.
At midnight, the innkeeper woke him.
The Fox and Cat had already left.
They had told Pinocchio to pay for dinner.
Pinocchio gave the innkeeper one gold coin.
Now their friendly story felt less true.
The innkeeper told him to reach the field alone.
Outside, the road was dark, narrow, and quiet.
The four coins made sounds in Pinocchio’s pocket.
Bare trees reached over the road like fingers.
The Talking Cricket sat on a low stone.
He looked pale, like a memory or ghost.
“Turn back and take the honest road,” he said.
Pinocchio said his friends would make him rich.
The Cricket said good friends paid their own bills.
For a moment, Pinocchio almost went home.
Then he felt ashamed about being tricked.
He walked forwards and ignored the Cricket.
Two dark shapes moved behind him.
They wore bags over their heads.
“Give us your money,” one shape ordered.
Pinocchio knew the Fox’s walk and Cat’s voice.
He quickly put the four coins into his mouth.
The thieves caught him and pulled at his jaw.
Pinocchio closed his teeth and kicked hard.
One thief held his arms tightly.
The other thief reached near his mouth.
Pinocchio bit the paw, and the Cat cried out.
He pulled free and ran into the forest.
The two thieves followed close behind.
Pinocchio crossed water and jumped over roots.
The coins hit together behind his teeth.
Then he saw a small white house.
A child with blue hair stood at a window.
Pinocchio asked her to open the door quickly.
She said nobody alive was inside the house.
The window closed, and Pinocchio was alone.
He knocked again, but nobody opened the door.
The two thieves came into the garden.
They asked for the coins one more time.
Pinocchio could not speak with a full mouth.
They searched his clothes and wooden body.
But the thieves did not find the gold.
They decided to frighten him.
They tied Pinocchio to a long rope.
Then they lifted him into a large oak tree.
Strong wind and rain moved the branches.
The thieves waited for Pinocchio to give up.
But he kept the coins inside his mouth.
Soon the thieves became cold and tired.
They promised to return with tools at sunrise.
Then they left Pinocchio alone in the storm.
The rope held him while the wind turned him.
He stopped thinking about the golden tree.
He thought about Geppetto waiting at home.
He remembered the coat and the lost book.
Every easy choice had taken him farther away.
“Father,” he tried to call through the coins.
Lightning showed the white house again.
A small blue light appeared behind one window.
Someone inside had finally seen him.
Pinocchio became weak as the storm continued.
His hands opened, and his head fell forwards.
The coins now felt empty and useless.
Then he heard large wings above the tree.
A great bird flew around the oak.
It called loudly towards the white house.
A door opened as the blue light grew.
At last, help was coming towards Pinocchio.
But could it reach him before sunrise?
