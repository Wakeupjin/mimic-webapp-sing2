Fire Eater opened his leather purse and counted five wonderfully bright gold coins.
“Carry these to your father,” he instructed Pinocchio in his roughest voice.
That small fortune promised food, firewood, books, and another much-needed winter coat.
Pinocchio thanked him with such feeling that the giant immediately sneezed.
At sunrise, the puppet left the theatre and turned eagerly towards home.
One protective hand covered his pocket while he imagined Geppetto’s astonished face.
Each step made the hidden coins ring like five separate promises.
He would replace the primer, stock the cupboard, and make their room comfortable.
No drum, painted sign, or passing amusement would divert him this time.
He had almost crossed the square when two elegant travellers offered a greeting.
One was a Fox with a limp; the other, a Cat claiming blindness.
Their smiles appeared polished and welcoming, although they arrived suspiciously quickly.
The Fox asked what could make a poor wooden boy look so prosperous.
Eager to share his good fortune, Pinocchio revealed the five coins.
The Cat’s blind eyes discovered their golden brightness with remarkable accuracy.
The Fox then described a distant country containing the Field of Wonders.
In that marvellous soil, he explained, a buried coin became a golden tree.
By morning, heavy branches would glitter beneath a harvest of new money.
Pinocchio instantly pictured Geppetto resting inside a warm, handsome house.
The Fox’s gentle voice made temptation sound remarkably like experienced advice.
“Why labour slowly when fortune is prepared to multiply?” he asked.
From an overhead branch, a Blackbird urged Pinocchio to distrust convenient miracles.
“Honest wealth does not grow merely because a stranger promises it,” the bird warned.
The Cat sprang upwards, ending that useful sentence in a violent flutter of feathers.
With injured dignity, the Fox complained that birds suspected everybody.
Unease touched Pinocchio, but his imagined golden tree shone more brightly.
He agreed to accompany the strangers for one evening and nothing more.
They assured him that the miraculous field lay practically on his road home.
Night had fallen when the three travellers reached the famous Red Lobster Inn.
The Fox declared that careful investors required a magnificent meal before working.
He ordered fish, meat, puddings, vegetables, and nearly everything the kitchen possessed.
The Cat ate heroically while pretending that blindness made every mouthful difficult.
Pinocchio requested only nuts, determined to protect most of Geppetto’s money.
Between enormous bites, his companions praised such admirable financial discipline.
Afterwards, they recommended sleeping until midnight, when secrecy would protect their investment.
The moon, they explained, guided wise travellers better than ordinary daylight.
Pinocchio hid the coins beneath his pillow and surrendered to golden dreams.
In those dreams, every branch bent beneath the weight of Geppetto’s happiness.
At midnight, the innkeeper woke him with a bill and an empty corridor.
His companions had departed early after receiving an urgent and mysterious message.
Naturally, they had entrusted their young friend with paying for the feast.
Pinocchio surrendered one precious coin and heard the first crack in their story.
The innkeeper repeated instructions to reach the field alone before sunrise.
Outside, a moonless road stretched between silent trees and tightly sleeping houses.
The four remaining coins sounded painfully loud inside Pinocchio’s pocket.
Bare branches leaned across the lane like fingers searching for hidden treasure.
Upon a low stone sat the pale shape of the Talking Cricket.
Whether ghost, memory, or conscience, he spoke with the same steady patience.
“Turn back now and choose the honest road to your father,” he urged.
Pinocchio insisted that the Fox and Cat were preparing to make him wealthy.
The Cricket observed that faithful friends rarely vanish before settling their bill.
For several uncertain steps, Pinocchio almost obeyed that sensible warning.
Then pride suggested that returning would mean confessing he had been deceived.
He continued alone, refusing to acknowledge the Cricket’s disappointed final sigh.
Far behind him, two narrow shadows separated themselves from the trees.
Dark sacks concealed their faces, and neither figure carried a lantern.
“Hand over your money or face the consequences,” one demanded.
Pinocchio recognised the Fox’s limp and the Cat’s rough, familiar whisper.
He answered by pushing all four coins deep inside his mouth.
The disguised thieves caught him and ordered his wooden jaws apart.
Pinocchio clenched his teeth and kicked with desperate, unexpected strength.
One attacker pinned his arms while the other pulled fiercely at his mouth.
Pinocchio bit an approaching paw, producing a distinctly feline howl.
He twisted free and plunged between the trees without choosing a path.
The assassins followed through thorns and roots, always frighteningly close behind.
Pinocchio crossed a ditch, climbed a bank, and leapt over twisted roots.
Whenever his pace weakened, the coins knocked together behind his teeth.
At last, a small white house appeared among the black trees.
A blue-haired child watched from an upper window, pale in the moonlight.
Pinocchio begged her to open the door before his pursuers reached him.
She answered quietly that nobody living remained anywhere inside the house.
The window closed, leaving him alone with rain and approaching footsteps.
He hammered again, but even desperate knocking failed to move the door.
The two masked figures entered the garden and surrounded their exhausted victim.
Once more, they demanded the coins.
Pinocchio attempted an answer, but his full mouth produced only muffled sounds.
They searched his jacket, cap, shoes, and the spaces between every joint.
Finding nothing, the thieves decided that greater fear might loosen his secret.
They tied him to a rope and raised him into a huge oak.
Wind bent the branches while cold rain erased the clearing below.
The assassins waited beneath him, confident that he would soon surrender.
Pinocchio kept his mouth shut, refusing to make cruelty profitable.
Minutes lengthened, and the thieves became wet, cold, and impatient.
They promised to return at dawn with tools suited to stubborn wood.
Then they vanished into the forest, abandoning him to the storm.
The rope held his wooden body while each gust turned him slowly.
Thoughts of golden trees and effortless wealth had finally disappeared.
He imagined Geppetto waiting beside the bare cupboard and painted fire.
The sold coat, lost primer, and delayed return formed one painful chain.
Every shortcut had carried Pinocchio farther from the father he meant to help.
“Father,” he tried to call, but gold still blocked his voice.
Lightning exposed the distant white house for one brilliant second.
Behind a shutter, a narrow blue light had begun to glow.
Someone had noticed him, although no rescuer entered the rain yet.
The long struggle weakened Pinocchio as the storm continued towards morning.
His wooden fingers loosened, and his head sank heavily forwards.
He still possessed the coins, but their promise had become utterly worthless.
Just before darkness claimed him, enormous wings beat above the oak.
A great bird circled once, then carried an urgent cry towards the house.
The blue light widened as a hidden door opened below.
For the first time that night, help was travelling in his direction.
Whether it could reach him in time would be settled before sunrise.
