Fire Eater opened his purse and counted five gold coins.
“Take these to your father,” he told Pinocchio with a gruff voice.
The coins could buy food, firewood, and another coat.
Pinocchio thanked him so warmly that the giant immediately sneezed.
At sunrise, Pinocchio left the theatre for home.
He kept one hand over his pocket and imagined Geppetto’s astonishment.
Every step seemed to ring with five small promises.
He would replace the primer, fill the cupboard, and repair the room.
This time, no drum or painted sign would turn him aside.
He had nearly crossed the square when two travellers greeted him.
One was a limping Fox, and the other was a blind Cat.
Their smiles were friendly, polished, and slightly too quick.
The Fox asked why a poor puppet carried such a cheerful expression.
[smoothly, with a trace of suspicion]
Pinocchio proudly revealed that he owned five gold coins.
The Cat suddenly appeared able to see their sparkle remarkably well.
The Fox described a distant place called the Field of Wonders.
There, he claimed, a planted coin would grow into a golden tree.
By morning, every branch would bend beneath new coins.
Pinocchio pictured Geppetto living in a warm and beautiful house.
The Fox spoke as though offering wisdom instead of temptation.
“Only fools work slowly when fortune can multiply overnight,” he said.
A Blackbird overhead warned Pinocchio not to trust convenient miracles.
“Honest money does not grow because a stranger tells it to,” it called.
The Cat sprang upwards, and the warning ended in a flutter of feathers.
The Fox sighed that some birds were hopelessly suspicious.
Pinocchio felt uneasy, yet the imagined golden tree shone brighter.
He agreed to travel with his new companions for only one evening.
They promised that the Field of Wonders was almost on the way home.
[with sly humour]
By nightfall, the three travellers reached the Red Lobster Inn.
The Fox announced that wise investors needed an excellent supper.
He ordered fish, meat, bread, puddings, and every expensive dish available.
The Cat ate silently while pretending to struggle.
Pinocchio requested only a few nuts because he wanted to save money.
His companions praised his discipline between enormous mouthfuls.
After dinner, they said everyone should sleep until midnight.
They would leave when the moon made secret travelling safer.
Pinocchio placed the coins beneath his pillow and closed his eyes.
Dreams of golden branches filled the dark room.
[uneasily]
At midnight, the innkeeper woke him with an empty bill and bad news.
The Fox and Cat had left early because of an urgent message.
Naturally, they had asked their young friend to pay for dinner.
Pinocchio surrendered one coin and felt the first crack in their story.
The innkeeper repeated their instruction to meet at the field before dawn.
Outside, the road was black, narrow, and completely silent.
The four coins seemed suddenly loud inside his pocket.
Bare branches reached over the lane like long, searching fingers.
A pale Cricket appeared on a low stone beside the path.
Whether ghost or memory, the adviser spoke with the same calm voice.
“Turn back now, and take the honest road home,” he urged.
Pinocchio insisted that the Fox and Cat were waiting to make him rich.
The Cricket reminded him that good friends did not vanish before paying.
For several steps, Pinocchio nearly followed the warning.
Then pride whispered that returning home would prove he had been fooled.
He continued alone and pretended not to hear the Cricket’s final sigh.
