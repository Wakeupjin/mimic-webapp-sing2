[with rising suspense]
Far behind him, two shadows slipped from the trees.
They wore dark sacks over their heads and carried no lanterns.
“Your money or your life,” one shadow demanded.
Pinocchio recognised the Fox’s limp and the Cat’s broken whisper.
Instead of speaking, he pushed all four coins into his mouth.
The thieves grabbed him and ordered him to open it.
Pinocchio clenched his wooden teeth and kicked wildly.
One attacker seized his arms while the other pulled at his jaw.
Pinocchio bit a paw, and the thief howled like an offended cat.
He tore himself free and raced into the forest.
The assassins followed, stumbling through thorns but never falling far behind.
Pinocchio crossed a ditch, scrambled up a bank, and leapt over roots.
Whenever he slowed, the coins struck together behind his teeth.
[hushed and urgent]
At last, he saw a small white house among the trees.
A blue-haired child appeared at an upper window, pale as moonlight.
Pinocchio begged her to open the door before the assassins arrived.
The child quietly answered that nobody in the house was alive.
Then the window closed, leaving Pinocchio alone with the wind.
He knocked again, but even his desperate fists received no answer.
The two dark figures reached the garden and surrounded him.
They demanded the coins one final time.
Pinocchio tried to explain, but he could not speak with a full mouth.
The thieves searched his jacket, shoes, cap, and every wooden joint.
When they found nothing, they decided to frighten the truth from him.
[solemnly]
They tied a rope around him and raised him into a great oak tree.
The storm bent the branches while rain swept across the clearing.
The assassins waited below, certain that Pinocchio would surrender the coins.
Yet he kept his mouth closed and refused to reward their cruelty.
Minutes passed, and the thieves grew cold and impatient.
They promised to return at dawn with better tools.
Then they disappeared between the trees, leaving the puppet in darkness.
[quietly, then hopefully]
The rope held his wooden body while the wind spun him slowly.
His thoughts no longer visited golden trees or sudden wealth.
He thought of Geppetto waiting beside an empty cupboard.
He remembered the sold coat, the lost primer, and the promised return.
Every easy choice had carried him farther from the person he loved.
“Father,” he tried to call, but the coins blocked his voice.
Lightning flashed across the branches, revealing the white house again.
Behind one shutter, a narrow line of blue light appeared.
Someone inside had seen him, though no rescue came at once.
Pinocchio’s strength faded as the storm continued through the night.
His wooden hands loosened, and his head dropped against his chest.
The coins remained hidden, but their promise now seemed painfully empty.
Just before the world went dark, he heard the beat of enormous wings.
A great bird circled the oak and called towards the house.
The blue light widened, and a distant door opened.
For the first time that night, help was moving towards him.
Whether it arrived soon enough would be decided before sunrise.
