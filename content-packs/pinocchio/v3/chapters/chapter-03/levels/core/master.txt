Pinocchio left home proudly, carrying the alphabet primer beneath his arm.
He imagined learning quickly and bringing Geppetto comfort in old age.
First he would read, then write, then master every number.
One day, he might earn enough to buy back the winter coat.
These excellent plans marched beside him along the road to school.
For several streets, Pinocchio walked with the seriousness of a scholar.
Then a lively burst of drums and pipes floated over the rooftops.
His feet slowed, although he told them firmly to continue forwards.
“School lasts all day, but music may vanish,” he reasoned.
He decided to listen for only one minute and then hurry back.
The sound led him away from school and into a crowded square.
Bright flags danced above a travelling puppet theatre.
Already, the alphabet primer felt heavier beneath his arm.
A painted sign promised battles, laughter, heroes, and astonishing surprises.
Pinocchio pushed through the crowd and reached the ticket booth.
The ticket cost four pennies, but he did not own one.
He offered his paper jacket, his bark shoes, and his bread cap.
The ticket seller refused every strange piece of clothing.
Pinocchio looked down at the primer Geppetto had bought with his coat.
For one sharp moment, he remembered his father shivering at home.
Then the drums rolled again, and the promise of excitement won.
A boy bought the primer, and Pinocchio exchanged sacrifice for a ticket.
He rushed inside before regret could catch him.
Rows of children faced a tiny stage covered by a red curtain.
Pinocchio squeezed onto a bench as the lamps grew dim.
The curtain rose upon Harlequin and Punchinello arguing in a painted garden.
Their wooden arms waved, and their painted mouths traded ridiculous insults.
The audience laughed until Harlequin suddenly stopped in the middle of a line.
He leaned beyond the footlights and stared directly at Pinocchio.
“Look, brothers and sisters,” he cried, “one of our own has come!”
Every puppet onstage turned towards the wooden boy.
They abandoned the play, scrambled into the audience, and lifted him high.
Pinocchio had never been welcomed with such immediate, noisy affection.
The puppets called him cousin, brother, traveller, and honoured guest.
Together they danced while the musicians played faster and faster.
Children clapped, though nobody understood why the story had stopped.
For a few glorious minutes, Pinocchio forgot school, money, and consequences.
Then a heavy boot struck the floor behind the curtain.
The music broke off, and every puppet froze.
The theatre belonged to a huge man known as Fire Eater.
His black beard reached the ground and moved like a dark curtain.
His eyes were red, his voice thunderous, and his whip cracked loudly.
“Who has ruined my performance?” he demanded from the wings.
The puppets pointed nervously towards Pinocchio.
Fire Eater seized him by the collar and carried him backstage.
Pinocchio’s courage disappeared as quickly as the theatre music.
Behind the scenery, a great fire was roasting the owner’s supper.
Unfortunately, the flames were dying before the meat was cooked.
Fire Eater examined Pinocchio as if measuring a bundle of firewood.
“A dry wooden puppet should burn beautifully,” he announced.
Pinocchio shook so hard that his joints knocked together.
He cried for Geppetto and described his father alone without a coat.
Fire Eater raised his eyebrows, but his terrible face did not soften.
Then he gave three enormous sneezes that shook the hanging scenery.
The puppets whispered that sneezing was his secret sign of compassion.
Pinocchio saw a small opening for hope and begged again.
Fire Eater grumbled that he could not eat an uncooked supper.
He decided to spare Pinocchio and burn Harlequin instead.
Two assistants dragged the cheerful puppet towards the weakening fire.
Harlequin called goodbye to his friends and tried to smile bravely.
The other puppets covered their faces, unable to watch.
Pinocchio realised that his rescue had merely moved the danger elsewhere.
Relief turned bitter, and he stepped between Harlequin and the flames.
“Burn me instead,” he said, though his voice trembled.
“I cannot save myself by letting an innocent friend suffer.”
Fire Eater stared at him as if seeing the puppet for the first time.
He ordered the assistants to place Harlequin beside Pinocchio.
“Do you understand what you are offering?” the theatre owner asked.
Pinocchio swallowed his fear and nodded.
He thought of Geppetto, who had surrendered his coat without complaint.
Perhaps courage, like kindness, required giving up something precious.
The fire cracked behind him, and the whole company waited.
Fire Eater’s beard quivered, his nose wrinkled, and another sneeze exploded.
“I cannot burn either of you,” he roared, wiping his eyes.
Instead, he ordered the cooks to finish supper without extra firewood.
Harlequin threw his arms around Pinocchio with grateful relief.
The other puppets cheered until the ropes above them trembled.
Even Fire Eater pretended not to enjoy their happiness.
He demanded silence, but his next sneeze ruined the command.
That night, the theatre became a feast instead of a tragedy.
The puppets shared bread, fruit, music, and stories behind the stage.
Pinocchio described Geppetto’s painted fire and the sold winter coat.
Each detail made Fire Eater’s fierce expression slightly less severe.
He asked where Geppetto lived and whether the old man worked alone.
Pinocchio answered honestly, without decorating the truth.
It was a small but important change after his excuses at home.
Fire Eater said nothing more and walked away into his private wagon.
Harlequin thanked Pinocchio for refusing an easy escape.
“Today you behaved like a true brother,” he said.
Pinocchio felt prouder of that sentence than of all the applause.
Still, the empty space beneath his arm reminded him of the primer.
He admitted that he had sold it and expected Geppetto’s disappointment.
Harlequin told him that brave choices could follow foolish ones.
The first mistake did not have to command the rest of the day.
Before dawn, the company performed a special dance in Pinocchio’s honour.
Fire Eater watched from the shadows, pretending to count the chairs.
When the final note ended, he called Pinocchio forwards.
“You caused chaos,” he said, “but you also showed courage.”
The giant reached into a heavy purse hidden beneath his beard.
Something inside it rang with the clear sound of coins.
Pinocchio wondered whether the dangerous theatre had one more surprise.
Outside, morning waited, along with the road back to Geppetto.
This time, he meant to carry home more than another promise.
