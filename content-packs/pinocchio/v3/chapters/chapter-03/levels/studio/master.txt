Pinocchio left home with the alphabet primer tucked proudly beneath his arm.
He pictured himself learning quickly and protecting Geppetto from future hardship.
First came reading, then writing, and finally the mysteries of arithmetic.
Perhaps one day his earnings could replace the coat sacrificed for this book.
Those honourable plans accompanied him like disciplined soldiers towards the schoolhouse.
For several streets, Pinocchio maintained the solemn pace of a devoted scholar.
Then drums, pipes, and bright cymbals sent their invitation across the roofs.
His feet slowed despite receiving firm instructions to continue in the proper direction.
“Lessons remain all day, whereas music disappears,” he reasoned with dangerous neatness.
One minute of listening seemed harmless, especially when he intended to hurry afterwards.
Following the melody, he turned away from school and entered a crowded square.
Colourful flags snapped above the entrance to a travelling puppet theatre.
The primer beneath his arm suddenly felt less like a gift than a burden.
A painted sign advertised heroes, battles, laughter, and marvels beyond description.
Pinocchio pressed through the excited crowd until he reached the ticket window.
Admission cost four pennies, an inconvenient sum for someone with none.
He offered his paper jacket, bark shoes, and ridiculous cap made from bread.
The seller examined each treasure and declined them without the slightest hesitation.
Pinocchio looked at the primer purchased through Geppetto’s cold and sacrifice.
For one honest instant, he saw his father shivering in the little room.
Then the drums rolled magnificently, drowning the quieter voice of memory.
A nearby boy bought the primer, allowing Pinocchio to exchange duty for spectacle.
He slipped inside before regret could follow him through the doorway.
Expectant children faced a miniature stage framed by a heavy red curtain.
Pinocchio found a seat just as the lamps dimmed to a golden glow.
The curtain revealed Harlequin and Punchinello quarrelling inside a painted garden.
Their wooden limbs flew about while their painted mouths delivered perfect nonsense.
The audience roared until Harlequin stopped halfway through a particularly foolish insult.
Leaning beyond the footlights, he stared in amazement at Pinocchio.
“Brothers and sisters, look!” he cried. “One of our family has arrived!”
Every puppet on the stage turned towards the unexpected wooden visitor.
They abandoned the performance, climbed into the audience, and carried him away.
Never before had Pinocchio received such instant, extravagant, unquestioning affection.
The company called him brother, cousin, traveller, and guest of honour.
They danced together while the musicians chased them with faster melodies.
Children applauded enthusiastically, although the abandoned plot made no sense whatsoever.
For several glorious minutes, Pinocchio forgot school, Geppetto, money, and responsibility.
Then one immense boot struck the boards somewhere behind the scenery.
The music stopped in mid-note, and every puppet became perfectly still.
The theatre belonged to an enormous showman whom everyone called Fire Eater.
His black beard swept the floor like a curtain pulled across daylight.
Red eyes, a cracking whip, and a thunderous voice completed the alarming picture.
“Who has destroyed my performance?” he demanded from the shadowed wings.
The frightened puppets pointed towards Pinocchio with one trembling movement.
Fire Eater caught the newcomer by his collar and carried him backstage.
Pinocchio’s celebrated courage disappeared as swiftly as the cheerful music.
Behind the painted scenery, a fierce fire struggled to roast the owner’s supper.
The flames were fading, however, before the meat had properly cooked.
Fire Eater inspected Pinocchio with the practical interest of a man choosing fuel.
“A dry wooden puppet should produce an excellent blaze,” he decided.
Pinocchio trembled until every loose joint knocked against the next.
He called for Geppetto and described the old man alone, poor, and coatless.
Fire Eater lifted one eyebrow, though his terrifying expression otherwise remained unchanged.
Then three gigantic sneezes shook dust from every hanging piece of scenery.
The company whispered that sneezing revealed compassion beneath their master’s temper.
Recognising a narrow possibility, Pinocchio begged for his life once more.
Fire Eater complained that raw supper was no reward for running a theatre.
He would spare Pinocchio, he announced, and use Harlequin for firewood instead.
Two assistants dragged the cheerful performer towards the hungry, weakening flames.
Harlequin said farewell to his friends while attempting an impossibly brave smile.
Around him, the other puppets covered their faces and lowered their heads.
Pinocchio understood that mercy for him had merely transferred suffering to someone innocent.
His relief soured, and he placed himself firmly between Harlequin and the fire.
“Use me instead,” he said, unable to prevent his voice from shaking.
“I will not purchase my safety with the life of a friend.”
Fire Eater regarded him as though an entirely different puppet had appeared.
He ordered both wooden boys to stand together before him.
“Do you understand the price of what you are offering?” he asked.
Pinocchio swallowed, imagined the flames, and nevertheless nodded.
He remembered how Geppetto had surrendered warmth without demanding praise.
Perhaps real courage, like love, began when something valuable was truly at risk.
The fire snapped behind him while the theatre waited in absolute silence.
Fire Eater’s beard trembled, his nose wrinkled, and another sneeze finally erupted.
“Neither of you shall burn,” he thundered, pretending not to wipe his eyes.
He ordered the cooks to finish his supper with the fuel they already possessed.
Harlequin embraced Pinocchio so fiercely that both of them nearly toppled over.
The company cheered until ropes, scenery, and painted stars shook overhead.
Even Fire Eater appeared pleased, although his scowl argued otherwise.
He demanded complete silence, then spoiled the command with one further sneeze.
That night, the backstage rooms held a feast instead of an execution.
The puppets shared bread, fruit, music, and stories beneath the hanging scenery.
Pinocchio described Geppetto’s painted fire, empty cupboard, and missing winter coat.
Each truthful detail softened Fire Eater’s fierce face by an almost invisible degree.
He asked where the carpenter lived and whether he faced poverty alone.
Pinocchio answered plainly, resisting the familiar temptation to improve his own part.
After so many excuses, honest speech felt both difficult and surprisingly clean.
Fire Eater offered no comment and disappeared into his private wagon.
Harlequin thanked Pinocchio for refusing the easiest path to safety.
“Today you behaved as a true brother behaves,” he told him.
That quiet judgment pleased Pinocchio more deeply than the audience’s wild applause.
Even so, the absence beneath his arm reminded him of the primer.
He confessed the sale and imagined the disappointment waiting in Geppetto’s eyes.
Harlequin replied that one brave decision could still follow a foolish one.
A first mistake need not become commander of every choice that follows.
Before dawn, the whole company performed a joyful dance in Pinocchio’s honour.
Fire Eater watched from darkness while pretending to inspect the empty seats.
When the music ended, he summoned Pinocchio to the centre of the stage.
“You created chaos,” he said, “but you also displayed uncommon courage.”
From beneath his beard, the giant drew out a heavy leather purse.
Something inside rang with the unmistakable, hopeful sound of gold.
Pinocchio wondered what final surprise this dangerous theatre still contained.
Morning waited outside, and beyond it lay the road back to Geppetto.
This time, he hoped to bring his father something more solid than promises.
