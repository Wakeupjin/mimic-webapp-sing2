Pinocchio hurried home, certain Geppetto would be worrying beside the little lamp.
Instead, darkness filled the narrow room, and the painted fire offered no comfort.
He called for his father, but the bare walls returned only echoes.
From the cold hearth came a measured chirp, patient and strangely familiar.
A Talking Cricket emerged from the shadows and greeted him with solemn courtesy.
“Young people who refuse good advice often manufacture their own trouble,” he observed.
Pinocchio crossed his arms and dismissed advice as an invention for dull people.
He preferred eating, drinking, sleeping, and wandering wherever impulse carried him.
The Cricket warned that freedom without judgment usually arrived at hunger.
“Then I shall locate a generous dinner,” Pinocchio replied with easy confidence.
“Most dinners,” the Cricket noted, “are earned through work, skill, or study.”
The puppet stamped hard and commanded his unwelcome adviser to disappear.
The Cricket stayed, since necessary truths rarely choose a convenient moment.
Pinocchio’s temper surged ahead before his young judgment could catch it.
He snatched up a wooden mallet and flung it across the room.
The mallet struck the wall exactly where the Cricket had been standing.
After one fading chirp, the small adviser vanished into the surrounding darkness.
Silence settled heavily, and Pinocchio’s victory felt unexpectedly lonely.
He insisted aloud that he required nobody, least of all a lecturing insect.
His empty stomach answered with a growl fierce enough to embarrass him.
He searched Geppetto’s cupboard and discovered only dust and a bent spoon.
He inspected a jar, a basket, an old saucepan, and every forgotten corner.
Each container delivered the same merciless answer: there was nothing to eat.
Pinocchio blamed Geppetto, the Cricket, and a world arranged against him.
Blaming his own choices remained the one possibility he refused to examine.
Then a white egg appeared beneath the bench among a nest of shavings.
Hunger transformed that ordinary egg into an imaginary royal banquet.
He set out a pan before recalling that the painted fireplace produced no heat.
After searching again, he found a tiny brazier and coaxed it into life.
“At last, fortune has remembered my address,” he declared, breaking the shell.
Instead of yolk, a bright chick burst upwards with astonishing energy.
“Thank you for opening my door,” the little creature chirped.
It explained that its mother had hidden the egg safely in Geppetto’s room.
With a cheerful flutter, it crossed the window and vanished above the roofs.
Pinocchio regarded the empty shell, speechless with hunger and disbelief.
His magnificent supper had escaped without approaching either fire or pan.
For the first time, the Cricket’s warning sounded inconveniently accurate.
Nevertheless, pride prevented Pinocchio from admitting that anyone else was right.
He decided some kindly citizen would surely feed a starving wooden boy.
Outside, winter rain swept the lanes while every shutter remained firmly closed.
Pinocchio knocked at one house and requested a small piece of bread.
A sleepy man leaned from an upper window carrying an enormous basin.
“Stay where you are,” he called, and Pinocchio imagined steaming soup.
A heartbeat later, icy water crashed over the puppet’s wooden head.
He fled, shivering, as the window slammed and darkness returned.
At another doorway, a furious dog made the invitation equally clear.
Rain thickened around him, and each narrow lane appeared more hostile.
By midnight, a brave adventure had collapsed into a miserable search for shelter.
Pinocchio dragged himself home, soaked, exhausted, ashamed, and desperately hungry.
The Cricket had gone, leaving nobody to receive the apology now forming.
Pinocchio stretched his dripping feet towards the brazier’s last red glow.
He intended to rest for one brief minute before finding a solution.
Within moments, sleep pulled his chin down against his wooden chest.
While he dreamed, the quiet coals steadily consumed both unguarded feet.
Morning discovered him snoring beside two neat piles of cooling ash.
A pounding knock woke him, followed by Geppetto’s anxious voice.
Pinocchio sprang up in delight and immediately crashed onto the floor.
Looking down, he realised that both wooden feet had completely vanished.
“Open the door, my boy,” Geppetto called, unaware of the absurd disaster.
“I cannot,” Pinocchio wailed, “because somebody has eaten my feet!”
Geppetto suspected another trick and ordered him to stop being foolish.
Pinocchio attempted to crawl, but his rounded body rolled helplessly backwards.
Eventually, Geppetto climbed through the window, irritation changing instantly to concern.
He found his son stranded on the floor and forgot every prepared complaint.
Before demanding explanations, Geppetto gathered the frightened puppet into his arms.
Pinocchio released the night’s adventures in one tangled, breathless confession.
He mentioned the Cricket, the mallet, the egg, the rain, and the burned feet.
He also arranged several details so that innocence seemed to belong entirely to him.
Geppetto listened without interruption until the room became quiet again.
“Hunger has taught what patient advice could not,” he said without triumph.
From his pocket, he produced three modest pears intended for his own breakfast.
Pinocchio demanded peeled fruit, with every troublesome core carefully removed.
He devoured the soft pieces and immediately complained that they were insufficient.
Geppetto placed the discarded peels and cores upon the empty table.
Pinocchio protested, then finally noticed his father’s exhaustion and thin clothing.
Geppetto had returned from prison carrying food he could scarcely spare.
Slowly, Pinocchio ate the peels and then the cores, wasting nothing.
“I did not know plain food could taste so rich,” he confessed.
Geppetto smiled because gratitude, however late, had entered their poor home.
Pinocchio glanced at his missing feet and began to weep with genuine shame.
He promised to study faithfully, work cheerfully, listen carefully, and love his father.
Geppetto understood that promises required less effort than changed behaviour.
Yet love persuaded him to hope, and he selected two fresh pieces of wood.
He measured and carved while Pinocchio fought the urge to wriggle.
Before long, two smooth new feet waited side by side on the bench.
Geppetto fixed them securely and advised caution during their first use.
Pinocchio danced around the room, but for once he stopped when asked.
He embraced Geppetto and renewed his promise to become a serious pupil.
The following morning, Geppetto fashioned clothes from whatever poverty provided.
The jacket was paper, the shoes were bark, and the cap was bread.
Pinocchio admired the unusual outfit as proudly as any velvet suit.
Then he remembered that a proper student needed an alphabet primer.
Geppetto’s expression clouded because his pockets contained not a single coin.
“Remain here for a little while,” he said, fastening his winter coat.
He entered the freezing street as Pinocchio practised writing an uncertain name.
Much later, Geppetto returned with a bright new primer under his arm.
He trembled visibly, protected from winter by nothing but a thin shirt.
“Father, where is your coat?” Pinocchio asked, although understanding had arrived.
“It was far too warm,” Geppetto replied with an unsuccessful laugh.
Pinocchio held the precious book against the place where a heart might grow.
At last, he understood that love sometimes became visible through what was surrendered.
At sunrise, he walked towards school dressed entirely in Geppetto’s sacrifice.
Somewhere along that road, temptation was already tuning drums and pipes.
