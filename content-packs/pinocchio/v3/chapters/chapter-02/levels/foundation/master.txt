Pinocchio hurried home and hoped to see Geppetto there.
But the little room was dark, cold, and empty.
He called for his father, but nobody answered.
Then he heard a slow sound near the fire.
A Talking Cricket came out of the shadows.
“Bad choices often bring trouble,” the Cricket said.
Pinocchio crossed his arms and called advice boring.
He wanted to eat, sleep, and go anywhere.
The Cricket said that careless freedom could bring hunger.
“I will find someone who gives me dinner,” Pinocchio answered.
“People work or study for their food,” said the Cricket.
Pinocchio stamped his foot and told him to leave.
But the Cricket stayed and continued speaking honestly.
Pinocchio became angry before he stopped to think.
He picked up a wooden hammer and threw it.
The hammer hit the wall near the Cricket.
After one small sound, the Cricket disappeared.
The room became quiet, and Pinocchio felt alone.
He said that he did not need anyone.
Then his empty stomach made a very loud sound.
He searched Geppetto’s cupboard and found only dust.
He looked inside every jar, basket, and pan.
There was nothing to eat anywhere.
Pinocchio blamed Geppetto and the whole world.
He did not blame his own bad choices.
Then he found one white egg under some wood.
To the hungry puppet, it looked like a feast.
He put a pan beside the painted fire.
Then he remembered that the fire was not real.
He found a small heater and made a flame.
“Now I can eat,” he said, opening the egg.
But a small yellow chick jumped out.
“Thank you for opening my door,” it said.
The chick’s mother had hidden the egg there.
It flew through the window and over the roofs.
Pinocchio looked at the empty shell in surprise.
His dinner had escaped before reaching the pan.
The Cricket’s words now sounded more helpful.
Still, Pinocchio did not want to say so.
He decided that someone in town would feed him.
Outside, cold rain fell on the dark streets.
Pinocchio knocked on a door and asked for bread.
A tired man looked down from an upstairs window.
“Wait there,” he said, holding a large bowl.
Pinocchio hoped that the bowl held hot soup.
Instead, cold water fell all over his head.
He ran away while the window closed.
At another house, a large dog barked at him.
The rain became heavier, and the streets felt dangerous.
At midnight, Pinocchio stopped feeling brave.
He came home wet, tired, sorry, and hungry.
The Cricket was gone, so nobody heard his apology.
Pinocchio put his wet feet near the small fire.
He planned to rest for only one minute.
Soon, he was fast asleep.
The hot coals slowly burned his wooden feet away.
Morning found him beside two piles of ash.
Then Geppetto knocked loudly at the door.
Pinocchio jumped up and fell onto the floor.
He looked down and saw that his feet were gone.
“Please open the door,” Geppetto called.
“I cannot because someone ate my feet!” Pinocchio cried.
Geppetto thought that his son was playing a game.
Pinocchio tried to move, but he rolled backwards.
At last, Geppetto climbed through the window.
He saw Pinocchio on the floor and forgot his anger.
Geppetto picked him up before asking any questions.
Pinocchio quickly told him about the whole night.
He spoke about the Cricket, egg, rain, and fire.
He also gave many reasons for his own mistakes.
Geppetto listened until Pinocchio finished.
“Hunger taught you an important lesson,” he said.
Then Geppetto took three pears from his pocket.
The pears were going to be his breakfast.
Pinocchio asked him to remove the skins and cores.
He ate the soft fruit and wanted more.
Geppetto put the skins and cores on the table.
Pinocchio complained, but then he saw his tired father.
Geppetto had come from prison with very little food.
Pinocchio ate every skin and core.
“Simple food can taste very good,” he said.
Geppetto smiled because Pinocchio was finally thankful.
Then Pinocchio looked down and cried about his feet.
He promised to study, work, listen, and help Geppetto.
Geppetto knew that a promise was only a beginning.
However, he chose to trust his son again.
He carefully made two new feet from wood.
Pinocchio waited and tried not to move.
Soon, Geppetto fixed both feet in place.
Pinocchio danced, but he stopped when Geppetto asked.
He hugged his father and promised to attend school.
The next morning, Geppetto made simple clothes for him.
The jacket was paper, and the shoes were bark.
His funny cap came from a piece of bread.
Pinocchio felt as fine as a prince.
Then he remembered that pupils needed a schoolbook.
Geppetto had no money to buy one.
“Wait here,” he said, putting on his winter coat.
He went outside while Pinocchio practised writing.
Later, Geppetto returned with a new alphabet book.
He had no coat, and his thin shirt shook.
“Where is your coat?” Pinocchio asked quietly.
“It was too warm,” Geppetto answered with a weak laugh.
Pinocchio held the important book against his chest.
He understood that Geppetto had given up something precious.
At sunrise, Pinocchio started walking towards school.
Far ahead, exciting music waited beside the road.
