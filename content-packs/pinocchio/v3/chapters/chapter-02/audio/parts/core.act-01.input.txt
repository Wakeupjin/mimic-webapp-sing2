[British accent]
[with quiet unease]
Pinocchio raced home, expecting Geppetto beside the lamp.
Instead, the room was dark, cold, and empty.
He called for his father, but only the walls answered.
From a shadow near the hearth came a slow, patient chirp.
A Talking Cricket stepped forward and bowed gravely.
“Children who reject good advice often meet avoidable trouble,” he said.
Pinocchio folded his arms and called advice boring.
He wanted to eat, drink, sleep, and wander wherever he pleased.
The Cricket warned that such freedom could quickly become hunger.
“Then I shall simply find a generous dinner,” Pinocchio replied.
“People usually earn their dinners,” said the Cricket, “by work or study.”
The puppet stamped his foot and ordered the visitor to leave.
Yet the Cricket remained, because honest warnings are rarely convenient.
[sharply, then hollow]
Pinocchio’s anger rose faster than his judgment could follow.
He seized a wooden mallet and threw it across the room.
The mallet struck the wall where the Cricket had been standing.
After one final chirp, the small adviser disappeared into the darkness.
Silence filled the room, and Pinocchio suddenly felt less brave.
He told himself that he needed nobody, especially that scolding insect.
At that moment, his empty stomach growled like a restless dog.
He searched the cupboard and found only dust.
He opened a jar, a basket, and even an old saucepan.
Every container offered the same disappointing answer: absolutely nothing.
Pinocchio blamed Geppetto, the Cricket, and the unfairness of the world.
He did not yet think of blaming the choices he had made.
[with comic disappointment]
Then he noticed one white egg lying beneath a heap of shavings.
His hunger turned the ordinary egg into a magnificent feast.
He set a pan on the painted fire before remembering it was unreal.
After much searching, he found a tiny brazier and lit it.
“At last, fortune has remembered me,” he said, cracking the shell.
Instead of a golden yolk, a lively chick popped into the air.
“Thank you for opening the door,” it chirped cheerfully.
The chick explained that its mother had hidden the egg there.
Then it fluttered through the window and vanished across the rooftops.
Pinocchio stared at the empty shell with his mouth hanging open.
His grand dinner had escaped before reaching the pan.
For the first time, the Cricket’s warning sounded less like an insult.
Still, pride stopped Pinocchio from admitting that the Cricket was right.
[cold and tired]
He decided that strangers in town would surely feed a hungry boy.
Outside, rain swept through the streets and shutters were tightly closed.
Pinocchio knocked at one door and politely asked for a little bread.
A sleepy man appeared upstairs holding a large basin.
“Wait there,” the man called, and Pinocchio imagined hot soup.
A moment later, icy water poured over his wooden head.
He ran away shivering while the man slammed the window shut.
At another house, a dog barked so fiercely that he fled again.
The rain grew heavier, and each dark lane looked more unfriendly.
By midnight, Pinocchio’s brave adventure had become miserable.
He returned home soaked, hungry, exhausted, and ashamed.
The Cricket was gone, and there was nobody to hear his apology.
Pinocchio placed his wet feet on the brazier to warm them.
