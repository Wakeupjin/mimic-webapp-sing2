Pinocchio swam through the storm until the waves finally weakened.
At dawn, a current carried him towards a rocky island.
He crawled onto the shore, exhausted, shivering, and completely alone.
The sea offered no sign of Geppetto or his little boat.
Pinocchio called until a large Dolphin rose beside the rocks.
“Did you see my father in the storm?” he asked.
The Dolphin had seen a small boat struggling far from land.
Nearby, the enormous Shark had also been hunting through the waves.
Sailors feared that creature because it could swallow ships whole.
Pinocchio’s wooden heart seemed to sink inside his chest.
The Dolphin could not say whether Geppetto had escaped.
It pointed towards a road leading inland and wished him courage.
Pinocchio thanked the animal and began searching the unfamiliar island.
The road entered a town where everyone moved with busy purpose.
Shopkeepers lifted shutters while bakers carried steaming trays.
Builders stacked stone, children delivered parcels, and carts rattled past.
Pinocchio’s stomach growled at every loaf displayed in a window.
He asked a charcoal seller for a coin to buy breakfast.
The man offered four coins if Pinocchio helped push his heavy cart.
Pinocchio refused because the black dust might stain his clothes.
Next, he asked a mason carrying a basket of mortar.
The mason offered bread if Pinocchio carried the basket instead.
Again, the puppet refused because the work looked tiring.
Several more people offered honest tasks, but none offered free food.
The town seemed cruel only because everyone expected him to contribute.
By noon, pride felt heavier than any basket or cart.
Near a fountain, Pinocchio met a woman carrying two water jugs.
He begged her for one drink and swallowed the cool water gratefully.
Then the smell of food escaped from the basket on her arm.
“I will give you a meal,” she said, “if you carry one jug.”
Pinocchio stared at the steep road leading towards her home.
His arms already imagined the weight, and his pride protested.
Then his empty stomach answered more honestly than pride could.
He lifted the jug and followed the woman uphill.
The water sloshed, his feet slipped, and his shoulders began aching.
Still, he reached the house without spilling a single drop.
The woman served bread, cauliflower, and a small sweet for dessert.
Never had a simple meal tasted more generous or deserved.
Pinocchio discovered that work could satisfy more than hunger.
As he finished eating, Pinocchio studied the woman’s face.
Her eyes were familiar, and her hair shone deep blue.
“You look like someone I once knew,” he said carefully.
The woman smiled and asked whom he remembered.
Pinocchio described the little Blue Fairy who had saved his life.
“I am that Fairy,” she replied, “but I have grown older.”
While Pinocchio chased easy adventures, time had continued around him.
He threw his arms around her and called her his little mother.
The Fairy held him, yet she did not erase his past choices.
The memory of the memorial stone still made Pinocchio tremble.
His grief had been real, and so was his wish to change.
For the first time since the storm, Pinocchio felt safely at home.
He asked whether Geppetto might still be alive inside the wide world.
“Hope for him,” said the Fairy, “and become someone worth finding.”
Pinocchio begged her to turn him into a real boy immediately.
The Fairy explained that real childhood required more than human skin.
A son must learn, work, tell the truth, and care for others.
Pinocchio promised to attend school and practise those habits daily.
“Promises are beginnings,” she said, “but actions complete them.”
The next morning, she gave him books and sent him to class.
He walked there proudly, imagining Geppetto seeing him as a student.
Letters, numbers, and maps seemed difficult during the first lessons.
Whenever he wanted to escape, he remembered the farmer’s heavy collar.
He also remembered carrying water and earning his first honest meal.
Those memories kept him seated when attention became uncomfortable.
Soon, Pinocchio discovered that curiosity could be stronger than boredom.
Some pupils mocked his wooden head and called him a puppet.
At first, he answered every insult with another insult.
Then he learnt that a calm reply ended most quarrels faster.
He studied each evening and began finishing lessons before anyone else.
His teacher praised his effort rather than his clever answers.
Several classmates respected him, although a few became jealous.
The jealous boys preferred adventures that required no patience.
They teased Pinocchio whenever he chose homework over wandering.
Each good choice made the next one slightly easier.
One bright morning, the class prepared for an important lesson.
Before the bell, a group of boys whispered outside the school.
They claimed the Terrible Shark had appeared near the beach.
Pinocchio immediately thought of Geppetto and the vanished boat.
“Perhaps the creature knows what happened to my father,” he said.
The boys promised that the sea monster could be seen clearly.
They urged him to skip only one lesson and return quickly.
Pinocchio looked towards the classroom where his teacher was waiting.
He also looked towards the road that curved down to the sea.
The Fairy’s warning about actions echoed inside his mind.
Yet the thought of Geppetto inside the Shark silenced every caution.
“I shall look once, then come straight back,” he decided.
The boys exchanged smiles that Pinocchio did not notice.
They began running, and he followed them away from school.
The road dropped between yellow fields and silver olive trees.
With every step, the sound of the sea grew louder.
Pinocchio told himself that searching for his father excused the absence.
He did not ask why his companions laughed whenever he mentioned duty.
Soon the school roof disappeared behind the hill.
The beach opened ahead, bright and empty beneath the sun.
No dark fin broke the water, and no crowd watched the waves.
Only gulls circled above a calm, glittering sea.
Pinocchio searched the horizon and felt his hope beginning to cool.
“Where is the Shark?” he demanded.
The boys stopped laughing but offered no answer.
Their satisfied glances made the trap suddenly clear.
Pinocchio stared back towards the distant town.
One quick choice had carried him much farther than he intended.
The lesson he had skipped was no longer his greatest problem.
The boys began circling him, mocking his books and his promises.
At last, one boy stepped forward to explain why they had brought him there.
