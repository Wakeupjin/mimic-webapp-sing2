Pinocchio swam through the storm until the waves slowly surrendered their strength.
At dawn, a tired current carried him towards the dark outline of an island.
He crawled onto the rocks shivering, exhausted, and painfully alone.
Neither Geppetto nor the tiny boat interrupted the empty surface of the sea.
Pinocchio called repeatedly until a broad-backed Dolphin rose beside the shore.
“Did you see my father during the storm?” he asked breathlessly.
The Dolphin remembered a small boat struggling dangerously far from land.
It had also seen the immense Shark hunting through those violent waters.
Sailors feared the creature because ships could disappear inside its mouth.
Pinocchio felt his wooden heart sink beneath the weight of that possibility.
The Dolphin could offer no certainty about whether Geppetto had survived.
Instead, it pointed towards an inland road and wished the boy courage.
Pinocchio thanked his guide, then began searching the unfamiliar island alone.
The road entered a town where purposeful movement filled every street.
Bakers balanced steaming trays while shopkeepers raised their painted shutters.
Builders stacked stone, children delivered parcels, and loaded carts rattled over cobbles.
Every loaf displayed behind glass seemed to call directly to Pinocchio’s stomach.
He approached a charcoal seller and requested one coin for breakfast.
The seller offered four coins if Pinocchio helped push his overloaded cart.
Pinocchio refused, imagining black dust settling permanently across his clothes.
Next, he appealed to a mason carrying a deep basket of mortar.
The mason offered bread in exchange for carrying the heavy basket.
Again, Pinocchio declined because honest work looked inconvenient and tiring.
Person after person offered employment, yet nobody offered a meal without effort.
The town seemed heartless only because it expected him to contribute.
By midday, Pinocchio’s pride weighed more than any cart or basket.
Near a fountain, he noticed a woman balancing two enormous water jugs.
She allowed him to drink deeply from the cool, shining water.
Then a delicious smell escaped from the covered basket resting on her arm.
“Carry one jug to my house,” she offered, “and you may share my meal.”
Pinocchio studied the steep road winding towards her distant doorway.
His shoulders imagined the burden while his pride prepared another refusal.
This time, however, his empty stomach answered with greater honesty.
He lifted the jug carefully and followed the woman uphill.
Water struck the sides, stones shifted, and pain spread across his shoulders.
Still, he reached her home without sacrificing a single precious drop.
The woman served bread, cauliflower, and one modest sweet for dessert.
After earned effort, the ordinary meal tasted like a royal banquet.
Pinocchio discovered that useful work could satisfy dignity as well as hunger.
While finishing his food, he began studying the woman’s face more closely.
Her eyes held a familiar patience, and her hair shone unmistakably blue.
“You remind me of someone I once loved,” Pinocchio said cautiously.
The woman smiled and invited him to describe this mysterious friend.
He spoke of the little Blue Fairy who had repeatedly saved him.
“I am that Fairy,” she replied, “although I have not remained little.”
While Pinocchio chased shortcuts, time had continued its ordinary journey.
He embraced her, calling her his little mother through sudden tears.
The Fairy returned his affection without pretending his choices had disappeared.
Even now, the memory of the memorial stone made Pinocchio tremble.
His grief had been genuine, and so was his imperfect desire to change.
For the first time since entering the storm, he felt securely at home.
He asked whether Geppetto might still be alive somewhere in the enormous world.
“Continue hoping,” said the Fairy, “and become the son he deserves to find.”
Pinocchio immediately requested transformation into a complete and ordinary human boy.
The Fairy explained that humanity required more than replacing wood with skin.
A real son must learn, work, speak honestly, and care beyond himself.
Pinocchio promised to practise those habits until they became part of him.
“Promises open the door,” she said, “but only actions carry us through.”
The following morning, she placed books in his arms and sent him to school.
He walked proudly, imagining the expression Geppetto might wear upon seeing him.
Letters, numbers, histories, and maps challenged him during those first lessons.
Whenever escape seemed attractive, he remembered the farmer’s humiliating collar.
He also remembered the water jug and the first meal he had honestly earned.
Together, those memories kept him seated when concentration became uncomfortable.
Before long, Pinocchio discovered that curiosity could outlast boredom.
Some pupils mocked his wooden head and dismissed every answer as a trick.
At first, Pinocchio fought each insult by returning one of his own.
Gradually, he discovered that a calm answer ended most quarrels sooner.
He studied each evening and often completed lessons before his classmates.
His teacher praised steady effort instead of admiring cleverness alone.
Several pupils began respecting him, although a jealous group remained.
Those boys preferred excitement that demanded no patience, work, or improvement.
They mocked Pinocchio whenever he chose homework over wandering through town.
Yet each responsible choice made the following choice slightly more natural.
One bright morning, the class prepared for a particularly important lesson.
Before the bell, several boys gathered outside and exchanged theatrical whispers.
They claimed the Terrible Shark had appeared near the beach.
At once, Pinocchio pictured Geppetto’s boat vanishing among the storm waves.
“Perhaps the monster can reveal what happened to my father,” he said.
The boys promised that its dark body could be seen from shore.
They urged him to miss a single lesson and return before anyone noticed.
Pinocchio looked towards the classroom, where his teacher was already waiting.
Then he looked towards the road descending between fields to the sea.
The Fairy’s warning about actions sounded quietly inside his thoughts.
Yet the possibility of Geppetto inside the Shark overwhelmed every caution.
“I shall look once and come directly back,” Pinocchio decided.
His companions exchanged victorious smiles that escaped his attention.
They began running downhill, and Pinocchio followed them away from school.
The road curved between yellow fields and rows of silver olive trees.
With every downward step, the breathing of the sea grew louder.
Pinocchio argued that searching for Geppetto surely excused one absence.
He never asked why his companions laughed whenever duty entered the conversation.
Soon, the school roof disappeared completely behind the rising hill.
The beach spread before them, bright, peaceful, and strangely empty.
No dark fin disturbed the water, and no frightened crowd watched the horizon.
Only gulls turned above a calm sea that glittered beneath the sun.
Pinocchio searched the distance while his urgent hope slowly cooled.
“Where exactly is this Shark?” he demanded at last.
The boys stopped laughing but withheld the truth for one final moment.
Their satisfied glances exposed the jealousy behind the invitation.
Pinocchio stared back towards the town now hidden behind the fields.
One quick exception had carried him much farther than he intended.
The missed lesson had already become the smallest part of his trouble.
They closed around Pinocchio, attacking his books, effort, and promises.
At last, their leader stepped forward to explain why they had brought him there.
