Morning found Pinocchio still trapped inside a donkey’s body.
Beside him, Lamp-Wick lowered his ears and trembled.
The Little Man burst in with a gentle smile.
He praised their braying as if their misery were excellent work.
He brushed their coats until they shone.
Neither boy could speak his thoughts.
Their human voices had vanished, leaving only rough and helpless cries.
The Little Man led them towards a distant market.
He had built his fortune by turning careless children into merchandise.
Lamp-Wick went to a farmer needing a strong animal.
A circus owner inspected Pinocchio’s legs, teeth, and eyes.
He paid and dragged Pinocchio away.
Pinocchio finally understood that endless play had carried a hidden price.
At the circus stable, Pinocchio was offered straw and dusty hay.
He refused both, expecting his earlier meals.
His new master answered his complaints with punishment and harder training.
Day after day, Pinocchio learnt to bow, dance, and count.
He balanced on his hind legs while music crashed around him.
He jumped through hoops and crossed narrow boards.
Whenever he hesitated, the trainer’s sharp command drove him onwards.
After weeks of practice, bright posters appeared throughout the town.
They promised an extraordinary donkey who could dance like a gentleman.
That evening, lamps glittered above a crowded ring.
Pinocchio entered wearing ribbons and a little hat.
To the crowd, he looked amusing, polished, and perfectly obedient.
Inside, he felt like a boy calling silently from behind a mask.
The orchestra began, and Pinocchio bowed exactly as he had practised.
He marched in a circle, counted beats, and balanced a glass.
Then his gaze reached a private box near the front.
A beautiful woman wore a necklace bearing his portrait.
Her blue hair shone softly beneath a silver veil.
Pinocchio knew at once that the Blue Fairy had found him.
Hope rushed through him so suddenly that he forgot the performance.
“Fairy, it is me!” he tried to cry across the ring.
Only a broken donkey’s bray came from his open mouth.
The audience laughed, believing this desperate call was another trick.
The Fairy’s eyes filled with sadness before she left.
Pinocchio watched the empty seat until the trainer shouted his name.
He faced the final jump with tears blurring the bright hoop.
Pinocchio ran, but his attention remained with the vanished Fairy.
His front feet struck the hoop, and his body fell heavily.
A fierce pain travelled through one leg when he hit the ground.
The music stopped, and the audience rose with a startled murmur.
The circus owner cared less about pain than the ruined performance.
By morning, a doctor had examined his swollen leg.
“He will always be lame,” the doctor told the manager.
The manager immediately decided that a useless performer should be sold.
At the market, buyers passed without offering even a coin.
At last, a drummer studied Pinocchio’s tough grey hide.
He did not need a friend, worker, or circus performer.
He wanted only strong leather for a new drumhead.
For four small coins, Pinocchio became his property.
The drummer led the limping donkey towards a cliff above the sea.
He tied a heavy stone around Pinocchio’s neck with thick rope.
Another rope tied one hind leg to the man’s hands.
Pinocchio pulled backwards, but his injured leg could not resist.
“Soon I shall have a splendid drum,” the man said cheerfully.
With one cold push, he sent Pinocchio over the edge.
Wind rushed past the donkey’s ears before the sea struck him.
The stone dragged him down through green water and silver bubbles.
Above, the drummer sat patiently, watching the rope disappear.
Below, Pinocchio sank into a silence deeper than the circus.
He thought of Geppetto and wished he had chosen home long ago.
Then hundreds of small fish flashed out of the dim water.
They nibbled away the hairy covering but spared the wood.
The strange feast continued until every trace of donkey skin disappeared.
Pinocchio felt his long ears, tail, and heavy body melt away.
Underneath them, his familiar wooden arms and legs remained unharmed.
He flexed his fingers and discovered that he could move freely again.
The Fairy stayed unseen, yet her protection surrounded him.
When the drummer finally pulled the rope, a marionette broke the surface.
The man stared at Pinocchio as though the sea had played a joke.
“Where is my donkey?” he demanded, still holding the wet rope.
“You are looking at him,” Pinocchio replied with a relieved laugh.
He explained the transformation while the drummer counted his lost coins.
The angry man reached forward, determined to capture him again.
Pinocchio slipped the loose rope from his ankle and dived away.
For the first time in months, his wooden limbs answered his will.
He swam towards open water, leaving the cliff and circus behind.
Waves lifted him gently while sunlight warmed his polished head.
Far ahead, a white rock rose above the glittering sea.
On it stood a small Goat whose coat was bright blue.
“Pinocchio, swim faster!” she called in the Fairy’s loving voice.
The puppet stretched both arms towards the rock and kicked harder.
Before he reached it, the water behind him began to roar.
Fish scattered in every direction as a vast shadow approached.
The Terrible Shark opened a mouth wider than a cave.
Its breath pulled water, foam, and Pinocchio towards rows of teeth.
The blue Goat called again, but her voice grew distant.
Pinocchio fought the current until his wooden joints seemed to crack.
One enormous rush lifted him from the sea and swallowed him whole.
He tumbled through darkness and landed in cold, oily water.
For several moments, he could hear only the monster’s deep breathing.
Pinocchio called for help, and a tired voice answered nearby.
A large Tunny floated beside him, waiting calmly in the blackness.
The fish explained that the Shark had swallowed many creatures before them.
He believed escape was impossible and preferred not to waste hope.
Pinocchio refused to accept a future inside someone else’s stomach.
As they argued, he noticed a faint golden point far away.
The tiny light flickered where no sunlight could possibly reach.
“Someone may know a way out,” Pinocchio whispered to the Tunny.
He thanked his new companion and began walking towards the glow.
Each step splashed through darkness, but the light slowly grew brighter.
For the first time, Pinocchio moved towards danger for a wiser reason.
He was no longer chasing amusement; he was searching for a way home.
