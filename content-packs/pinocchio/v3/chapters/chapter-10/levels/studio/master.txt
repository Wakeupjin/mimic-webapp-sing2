Morning found Pinocchio imprisoned in the grey body his choices had created.
Lamp-Wick stood beside him, ears lowered, unable to meet his friend’s eyes.
The Little Man burst in wearing the mild smile that concealed his trade.
He praised their braying as though their humiliation were a fine performance.
Then he groomed their rough coats until both animals shone for market.
The boys tried to protest, but human language had already abandoned them.
Every urgent thought emerged as a broken, helpless cry.
The Little Man led his newest merchandise towards a distant market square.
Toyland had made him wealthy by converting carelessness into saleable bodies.
A farmer quickly purchased Lamp-Wick to replace a donkey that had died.
Meanwhile, a circus owner examined Pinocchio with a professional, measuring gaze.
He tested the legs, inspected the teeth, and studied the unusual eyes.
Money changed hands, and Pinocchio was pulled away from his only friend.
At the circus stable, he received a manger filled with straw and hay.
Still thinking like a pampered child, Pinocchio refused the animal food.
His new master answered resistance with punishment, hunger, and relentless training.
Each day, the donkey learnt bows, dances, counts, and carefully timed turns.
He balanced upright while the orchestra repeated the same demanding tune.
He crossed narrow boards and sprang through hoops covered with thin paper.
Whenever fear slowed him, the trainer’s command forced his body onwards.
Within weeks, colourful posters spread Pinocchio’s strange fame across the town.
They promised a remarkable donkey who moved with the manners of a gentleman.
On opening night, lamps glittered above a ring crowded with excited families.
Pinocchio entered in bright ribbons and a ridiculous little hat.
Applause surrounded a polished animal while the trapped boy remained unseen.
He bowed obediently, carrying shame beneath every practised movement.
The orchestra began, and Pinocchio marched around the ring in perfect time.
He counted the beats, balanced a glass, and turned beneath the lights.
Then his attention caught on a private box near the front.
A graceful woman sat there wearing a necklace that carried his portrait.
Blue hair glimmered beneath her silver veil, unmistakable even across the ring.
Pinocchio recognised the Fairy, and his trained routine instantly disappeared.
Hope struck with such force that every command left his mind.
“Fairy, it is Pinocchio!” he tried to call above the music.
His mouth released only the harsh bray of a frightened donkey.
The audience laughed, mistaking an appeal for another comic trick.
Sadness crossed the Fairy’s face before she quietly left the box.
Pinocchio stared at the vacant seat until his master shouted sharply.
Through gathering tears, he faced the bright hoop prepared for the final jump.
He charged forward, but his thoughts remained with the vanished Fairy.
His front hooves struck the frame, throwing his body hard against the ground.
Pain travelled through one leg as the crowd’s delight turned into alarm.
The orchestra fell silent, and hundreds of voices became one uneasy murmur.
The circus owner saw a damaged investment rather than an injured creature.
By morning, an animal doctor had examined the swollen, useless leg.
“This donkey will remain lame,” he announced with practical certainty.
The owner immediately ordered the failed performer taken back to market.
Most buyers ignored an animal that could neither work nor entertain.
Finally, a drummer paused to test the strength of Pinocchio’s hide.
He needed no companion and expected no labour from the injured donkey.
He wanted durable leather for the head of a village drum.
Four small coins purchased the body that had recently drawn a cheering crowd.
The drummer led Pinocchio towards a high cliff overlooking the open sea.
He fastened a heavy stone around the donkey’s neck with thick rope.
A second rope tied one hind leg to the man waiting above.
Pinocchio struggled backwards, but pain had stolen the strength to resist.
The buyer cheerfully imagined the deep sound of his completed drum.
Then one deliberate push sent Pinocchio tumbling beyond the cliff.
Wind rushed past his long ears before the cold sea closed over him.
The stone dragged him down through green water filled with flashing bubbles.
Above the surface, the drummer settled patiently beside the secured rope.
Below, Pinocchio entered a silence deeper than any empty theatre.
He remembered Geppetto and finally understood the distance created by his choices.
Suddenly, hundreds of small fish appeared from the dim water.
They consumed the donkey covering while leaving the wooden body untouched.
The strange work continued until the grey hide had completely vanished.
Long ears, heavy hooves, and the humiliating tail dissolved into the sea.
Beneath them, Pinocchio’s familiar wooden limbs emerged safe and whole.
He opened his hands and discovered that movement belonged to him again.
Though unseen, the Fairy’s protection had reached him even here.
When the drummer hauled on the rope, a living marionette broke the surface.
The man stared as if the sea had exchanged his purchase for a joke.
“Where is the donkey I threw down?” he demanded in disbelief.
“You are looking at him,” Pinocchio replied, almost laughing with relief.
He explained the transformation while the drummer mourned his four lost coins.
The angry buyer lunged forward, determined to capture the puppet again.
Pinocchio freed his ankle, slipped from the rope, and dived beyond reach.
After months of helplessness, his wooden body answered every command.
He swam towards open water, leaving the cliff and circus behind.
Sunlight warmed his painted head as gentle waves carried him onwards.
Far ahead, a white rock rose alone from the glittering sea.
A small Goat with a brilliant blue coat stood upon it.
She called his name with the warmth and urgency of the Fairy.
Pinocchio stretched towards the rock and drove his tired limbs harder.
Before he reached safety, the water behind him began to roar.
Schools of fish scattered as a vast shadow climbed towards the surface.
The Terrible Shark opened jaws that seemed wider than a cave.
Its breath dragged foam, water, and Pinocchio towards rows of teeth.
The blue Goat continued calling, but her voice faded across the waves.
Pinocchio fought the current until every wooden joint ached with strain.
One irresistible surge lifted him from the sea and swallowed him whole.
He tumbled through darkness and landed in a pool of cold, oily water.
For several moments, only the monster’s breathing proved that time continued.
Pinocchio called into the blackness, and a weary voice answered nearby.
A large Tunny floated beside him with the stillness of surrender.
The fish described the many creatures already trapped inside the Shark.
Escape seemed impossible to him, so he had chosen to abandon hope.
Pinocchio refused to let another creature decide where his story ended.
While they argued, he noticed a faint golden point in the distance.
The light trembled where no ray from the outside world could enter.
“Someone there may know a path out,” Pinocchio told the Tunny.
He thanked his companion and began walking towards the uncertain glow.
Each step splashed through foul darkness, yet the light became clearer.
For once, Pinocchio was moving towards danger for a responsible purpose.
He no longer chased effortless pleasure; he was searching for home.
