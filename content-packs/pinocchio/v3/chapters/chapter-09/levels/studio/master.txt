The Fairy’s house glowed with preparations for Pinocchio’s long-awaited celebration.
Tomorrow, she promised, he would finally awaken as a real human boy.
A table waited beneath cakes, fruit, and carefully arranged sweets.
Twelve chairs stood ready for the school friends who had witnessed his progress.
Pinocchio felt proud because the promise now seemed earned rather than borrowed.
He hurried through town, inviting every classmate before the evening grew late.
Only Lamp-Wick, his closest and least responsible friend, remained impossible to find.
The boy’s nickname came from his thin body and restless, flickering energy.
Pinocchio searched streets, workshops, and playgrounds until daylight faded.
At last, he discovered Lamp-Wick hiding beneath the porch of a farmhouse.
“Come tomorrow and celebrate my new life,” Pinocchio said eagerly.
Lamp-Wick refused, glancing towards the road with poorly hidden excitement.
He was waiting for a midnight wagon that would carry him away.
Its destination was a famous place known as the Land of Toys.
According to Lamp-Wick, nobody studied, worked, or followed rules there.
The country had no schools, lessons, teachers, books, or early mornings.
Every day was a holiday, and play continued until children collapsed.
“I intend to live there forever,” Lamp-Wick announced with complete confidence.
Pinocchio remembered the Fairy’s party and the real body promised for morning.
He also imagined a life without correction, effort, or disappointing duties.
“I only came here to invite you,” he insisted, already sounding uncertain.
Lamp-Wick suggested that watching the wagon arrive could cause no harm.
One minute became ten while the moon climbed above the silent fields.
Pinocchio repeatedly promised himself that he would leave before departure.
Yet each bright detail made Toyland sound more difficult to resist.
By midnight, he still stood beside Lamp-Wick, listening for approaching bells.
A distant jingle reached them before the wagon’s lanterns entered view.
The enormous vehicle rolled slowly forward, overflowing with excited children.
No seat, step, or corner remained free inside the crowded carriage.
Twelve exhausted donkeys strained beneath the weight of their cheerful passengers.
Lamp-Wick climbed aboard while laughing children pressed closer together.
The driver invited Pinocchio to ride upon one donkey’s back.
Pinocchio hesitated, whispering that his own celebration began the next morning.
The animal beneath him turned slightly and spoke through its tight bridle.
“Leave now, before pleasure makes you a beast,” the donkey warned.
The driver struck the animal and pretended that no human voice had sounded.
Pinocchio saw fear and recognition trembling inside the donkey’s wet eyes.
Still, he climbed aboard while promising himself only one day of play.
The wagon disappeared into darkness, leaving twelve prepared chairs empty.
At sunrise, the children crossed into the celebrated Land of Toys.
Bells, drums, whistles, and delighted shouting poured through every street.
Children raced bicycles across squares while enormous paper kites filled the sky.
Theatres offered comedy from morning until night, and sweet shops never closed.
Nobody carried a schoolbag, repaired anything, or hurried towards useful work.
Pinocchio and Lamp-Wick threw themselves into every available amusement.
They built towers, broke toys, chased parades, and slept wherever they fell.
The next day offered the same pleasures, followed by another identical day.
Without clocks or duties, time lost its shape and promises became distant.
Occasionally, Pinocchio pictured the Fairy waiting beside her carefully prepared table.
Because the memory hurt, he buried it beneath louder games and laughter.
Toyland delivered every delight that Lamp-Wick had advertised so warmly.
It concealed only the delayed and terrible price.
Five careless months passed in a blur of games without completion.
One morning, Pinocchio awoke with a strange itching above his head.
His searching hands discovered two long ears covered with coarse hair.
At first, he blamed Lamp-Wick for another childish practical joke.
The mirror showed that the growing donkey ears belonged entirely to him.
Pinocchio forced them beneath an enormous cap and locked his door.
For several anxious hours, he searched for a harmless explanation.
Nothing in his familiar wooden body had ever produced fur before.
The ears continued lengthening until the cap could barely hide them.
Eventually, fear became too heavy for him to carry alone.
He called for medical help, and an elderly Marmot entered the room.
The Marmot understood the unusual illnesses that followed careless behaviour.
She examined his ears, pulse, hands, and increasingly unfamiliar feet.
Then she sighed with the gravity of someone delivering certain news.
“You have Donkey Fever,” she said, “and the change cannot be stopped.”
Children who rejected every responsibility eventually surrendered their childhood as well.
Within a few hours, Pinocchio would become a complete donkey.
He begged for medicine, surgery, punishment, or one final chance.
The Marmot offered sympathy because the transformation had begun months earlier.
Every ignored warning and repeated choice had quietly strengthened it.
Pinocchio remembered the speaking donkey harnessed beneath the midnight wagon.
He now understood that the exhausted animal had once been a child.
After the Marmot departed, Pinocchio hurried towards Lamp-Wick’s house.
His friend opened the door wearing a cap even larger than his.
Both boys kept their eyes lowered, afraid of recognising themselves.
Pinocchio asked Lamp-Wick to uncover first, but Lamp-Wick refused.
They finally counted to three and removed both caps together.
Matching pairs of donkey ears sprang upright above their frightened faces.
For one suspended moment, neither boy could produce a word.
Then fear escaped as wild laughter because the truth felt unbearable.
Their laughter roughened into brays that neither could control.
Hands hardened into hooves, faces lengthened, and clothes split apart.
Grey hair travelled across their bodies while tails emerged behind them.
The change ended with two terrified donkeys struggling upon the floor.
They had wanted freedom from effort and received freedom from choice.
Outside, the wagon driver heard their braying and smiled with satisfaction.
He entered carrying ropes and inspected the results of his investment.
Toyland had always been a machine for turning children into profitable animals.
Its glittering pleasures had merely disguised a carefully designed commercial trap.
Pinocchio tried to shout his name, but only another bray appeared.
Lamp-Wick answered with a helpless bray and kicked desperately against the ropes.
The driver tightened them and secured both boys beside each other.
Without language, the friends could only exchange terrified looks.
Pinocchio remembered the speaking donkey harnessed beneath the midnight wagon.
That exhausted creature had once been another child entering this trap.
He pictured the Fairy waiting beside her carefully prepared table.
Twelve empty chairs measured the promise he had abandoned.
Five careless months had transformed one short visit into a trap without an exit.
Beyond the locked room, another wagon arrived carrying laughing children.
Before dawn, the driver announced that the morning market would determine their fate.
No path back to the Fairy remained open before them.
Pinocchio’s almost-human tomorrow had become a morning he could not escape.
