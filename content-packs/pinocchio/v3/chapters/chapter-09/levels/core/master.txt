The Fairy’s house glowed with preparations for Pinocchio’s celebration.
Tomorrow, she promised, he would wake as a real boy.
Tables waited for sweets, and twelve chairs stood ready for friends.
Pinocchio felt proud of the patient work behind that promise.
He ran through town inviting every classmate to the party.
Only Lamp-Wick, his closest and laziest friend, could not be found.
People called the boy Lamp-Wick because he was thin and restless.
Pinocchio searched streets, workshops, and playgrounds until evening.
At last, he found Lamp-Wick hiding beneath a farmhouse porch.
“Come tomorrow and celebrate my new life,” Pinocchio said.
Lamp-Wick shook his head and glanced towards the road.
He was waiting for a midnight wagon and planned to leave town.
The secret made Pinocchio curious when he should have walked home.
Lamp-Wick described a place called the Land of Toys.
No schools, lessons, teachers, or alarm clocks existed there.
Every day was a holiday, and play continued from morning until night.
“I am going to live there forever,” Lamp-Wick announced.
Pinocchio remembered the Fairy’s party and his nearly human future.
He also imagined games without duties, correction, or difficult mornings.
“I only came to invite you,” he insisted.
Lamp-Wick replied that watching the wagon arrive would do no harm.
One minute became ten, and the moon climbed above the fields.
Pinocchio told himself he would leave before the passengers departed.
Yet every cheerful detail made Toyland sound more irresistible.
Lamp-Wick laughed at books, promises, and the idea of growing responsible.
By midnight, Pinocchio was still waiting beside him.
A distant jingle announced the wagon before its lanterns appeared.
It rolled slowly into view, packed with laughing children.
Twelve pairs of exhausted Donkeys pulled the enormous load.
There was no empty seat, step, or corner inside.
Lamp-Wick climbed onto the wagon as children squeezed together.
The driver invited Pinocchio to sit on a Donkey’s back.
Pinocchio hesitated and whispered that he must attend his celebration.
The Donkey beneath him spoke softly through its bridle.
“Turn back before pleasure turns you into a beast,” it warned.
The driver struck the animal and pretended no warning had sounded.
Pinocchio recognised fear in the Donkey’s wet eyes.
Still, he climbed aboard while promising himself a very short visit.
The wagon rolled away, leaving the Fairy’s twelve chairs empty.
At sunrise, the children entered the famous Land of Toys.
Its streets rang with bells, drums, whistles, and delighted shouting.
Some children raced bicycles while others flew enormous paper kites.
Theatres offered comedy all day, and sweet shops never closed.
Nobody carried a schoolbag or hurried towards useful work.
Pinocchio and Lamp-Wick plunged joyfully into every game.
They built towers, broke toys, chased parades, and slept whenever exhausted.
The next day offered the same freedom, then the next.
Without duties, time lost its shape and promises lost their voices.
Pinocchio occasionally remembered the Fairy waiting beside the prepared table.
Each memory hurt, so he covered it with louder amusement.
Toyland delivered exactly what it had advertised.
It concealed only the price.
Five months passed in a blur of games and unfinished days.
One morning, Pinocchio awoke with an uncomfortable itching above his head.
He reached up and felt two long, furry ears.
At first, he believed Lamp-Wick had played another joke.
The mirror showed that the ears belonged completely to him.
Pinocchio covered them with an oversized cap and locked his door.
For hours, he searched for a sensible explanation.
Nothing in his wooden body had ever grown fur before.
The ears lengthened until the cap could barely contain them.
His fear became too great to hide alone.
He called for help, and an old Marmot entered the room.
The Marmot had studied the strange illnesses of careless children.
She examined his ears, pulse, hands, and wooden feet.
Then she sighed with the seriousness of a doctor delivering bad news.
“You have Donkey Fever,” she said, “and it cannot be stopped.”
Children who rejected every duty eventually lost their childhood.
Within hours, Pinocchio would become a complete Donkey.
He begged for medicine, surgery, or one more chance.
The Marmot could offer only sympathy because the change had begun months earlier.
Every ignored warning had quietly strengthened the illness.
Pinocchio thought of the Donkey who had spoken beneath the wagon.
Now he understood that the animal had once been a child too.
After the Marmot left, Pinocchio hurried towards Lamp-Wick’s house.
His friend answered the door wearing an enormous cotton cap.
Both boys avoided looking directly at the other.
Pinocchio asked Lamp-Wick to remove his cap first.
Lamp-Wick refused and demanded the same from Pinocchio.
At last, they counted to three and uncovered their heads together.
Two matching pairs of Donkey ears sprang upright.
For one nervous moment, neither boy spoke.
Then they laughed wildly because terror felt impossible to admit.
Their laughter became rough brays that neither could control.
Hands hardened into hooves, faces lengthened, and clothes tore away.
Tails grew behind them as they stumbled around the room.
The transformation ended with two frightened Donkeys on the floor.
They had wanted freedom from effort and received freedom from choice.
Outside, the wagon driver heard their braying and smiled.
The driver entered with ropes and examined his newest merchandise.
He had brought children to Toyland precisely for this profitable harvest.
Toyland’s games had always been bait for a profitable trap.
Pinocchio tried to shout his name, but only a bray emerged.
Lamp-Wick answered with another helpless bray and kicked at the ropes.
The driver tightened them and tied both boys side by side.
Unable to speak, they could only meet each other’s frightened eyes.
Pinocchio remembered the speaking Donkey beneath the midnight wagon.
That exhausted animal had once been another careless child.
He pictured the Fairy waiting beside her carefully prepared table.
Twelve empty chairs now measured the promise he had abandoned.
Five playful months had become a trap without an easy exit.
Beyond the locked room, another wagon arrived with laughing children.
Before dawn, the driver announced that the morning market would decide their fate.
No road back to the Fairy remained open before them.
Pinocchio’s almost-human tomorrow had become a morning he could not escape.
