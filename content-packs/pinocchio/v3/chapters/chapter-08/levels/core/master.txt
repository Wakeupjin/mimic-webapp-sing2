The boys admitted that no Shark had appeared near the beach.
They had invented the rumour simply to pull Pinocchio from school.
His success made their own laziness impossible to ignore.
“Go back to your lessons,” Pinocchio said, turning away.
One boy snatched his cap and threw it across the sand.
Another knocked the schoolbooks from beneath his arm.
Pinocchio tried to leave, but they formed a laughing circle.
They called him a teacher’s pet and a painted wooden saint.
His careful patience began to crack under their insults.
“Return my things, and this ends now,” he warned.
Instead, the tallest boy kicked one book towards the water.
Pinocchio seized it before the next wave could carry it away.
When he stood again, anger had replaced every sensible intention.
The boys opened their own books and used them like weapons.
Grammar, arithmetic, and history flew across the beach in wild arcs.
Pinocchio dodged a dictionary and hurled an atlas back.
None of them remembered that books were meant to open minds.
Pages tore loose and fluttered over the sand like frightened birds.
During the struggle, a heavy textbook struck a boy named Eugene.
He fell without moving, and a dark bruise appeared on his head.
The noise stopped at once.
Pinocchio dropped the book in his hands and knelt beside Eugene.
“Help me carry him,” he begged the others.
Afraid of punishment, every boy ran towards the dunes.
Pinocchio remained, although staying made him look responsible.
He pressed his handkerchief against Eugene’s wound and called for help.
Two police officers heard Pinocchio’s cries and hurried down the beach.
They found a wooden puppet beside an injured child and scattered books.
Pinocchio explained the fight and named the boys who had fled.
Unfortunately, one of his schoolbooks lay closest to Eugene.
The officers assumed it had delivered the blow.
They praised Pinocchio for staying, then arrested him anyway.
“Waiting for the truth would be more useful,” Pinocchio protested.
As they marched towards town, they noticed his remarkable nose.
One officer worried that such a nose would attract a crowd.
A sudden sea wind lifted Pinocchio’s cap and sent it rolling away.
“Please let me fetch it,” he asked, and one officer loosened his grip.
Pinocchio recovered the cap, then raced along the shore.
The officers released their mastiff, Alidoro, to catch him.
Alidoro’s long legs swallowed the distance between them.
Pinocchio reached the water and ran straight into the waves.
Being wooden, he floated easily and began swimming beyond the breakers.
The heavy dog followed, driven by training rather than good judgement.
Soon Alidoro’s paws weakened, and his head slipped beneath the surface.
He called to Pinocchio, promising not to arrest him if rescued.
Pinocchio could have continued towards freedom.
Instead, he remembered Geppetto’s boat disappearing in the storm.
He turned back, caught Alidoro’s collar, and pulled towards shore.
The current fought them, but Pinocchio refused to release the dog.
At last, a wave rolled both of them onto the sand.
Alidoro coughed, breathed, and looked at his former prisoner differently.
“A rescued life creates a debt,” he said before limping away.
Pinocchio entered the sea again to avoid the returning officers.
A strong current carried him around a headland and out of sight.
There, a vast fishing net rose suddenly beneath his feet.
Pinocchio tumbled among silver fish, crabs, and twisting eels.
The net belonged to a strange Green Fisherman with seaweed hair.
He examined Pinocchio and decided he was a rare wooden fish.
“Whatever you are, you will taste better fried,” he announced.
He carried the net into a smoky cave beside the water.
One by one, he coated the fish with flour for his enormous pan.
Pinocchio explained that he was a puppet, not anyone’s supper.
The Fisherman only laughed and rolled him through the flour.
White dust covered Pinocchio from nose to heel.
Oil hissed over the fire while the Fisherman lifted him by one leg.
At that moment, angry barking echoed through the cave.
Alidoro had followed Pinocchio’s scent around the headland.
He sprang between the Fisherman and the pan.
While the startled man protected his dinner, Alidoro seized Pinocchio.
The dog carried him outside and raced along the rocks.
Pinocchio brushed flour from his face and thanked his rescuer.
“Today we each pulled the other from danger,” Alidoro replied.
He had also heard that Eugene was recovering from the injury.
The schoolboys had confessed, so the officers no longer blamed Pinocchio.
Relief warmed him, but shame about the fight remained.
He had been innocent of the worst harm, not of every mistake.
Alidoro left him near the road, and Pinocchio returned home alone.
Night had fallen when Pinocchio reached the Fairy’s tall front door.
He knocked softly, then loudly, then with both fists.
After a long delay, a Snail answered from an upstairs window.
She promised to come down, but snails measure urgency differently.
Hours passed while Pinocchio paced, pleaded, and shivered outside.
At last, anger defeated him, and he kicked the wooden door.
His foot broke through a panel and became firmly stuck.
Now he could neither enter nor walk away.
The Snail finally arrived at sunrise and found him exhausted.
She brought a breakfast that looked delicious from a distance.
The bread was plaster, the chicken was cardboard, and the fruit was stone.
Pinocchio’s hunger turned the joke into a painful lesson.
He fainted from tiredness and awoke safely inside the house.
The Fairy listened to the whole story without interrupting him.
Pinocchio admitted the skipped lesson, the fight, and his impatient kick.
He did not hide behind the lies or mistakes of his classmates.
The Fairy repaired his foot but left responsibility with him.
Over the following months, Pinocchio returned faithfully to school.
He studied, helped at home, and chose better friends.
When others teased him, he remembered how quickly anger could spread.
His teacher saw steady effort replacing bursts of enthusiasm.
At the end of the year, Pinocchio finished first in his class.
The Fairy announced that tomorrow he would become a real boy.
She planned a celebration and allowed him to invite his companions.
Pinocchio thanked her, already imagining Geppetto’s joyful face.
Before the party, he wanted to find every friend personally.
He hurried into town searching for Lamp-Wick, the laziest boy at school.
The future seemed wonderfully close, but temptation was waiting after dark.
