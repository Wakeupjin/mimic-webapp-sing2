The boys finally admitted that no Shark had appeared anywhere near the beach.
They had invented the rumour solely to drag Pinocchio away from school.
His progress reflected their laziness too clearly for their comfort.
“Return to your lessons,” Pinocchio said, turning towards the road.
One boy snatched his cap and sent it spinning across the sand.
Another struck the schoolbooks from beneath Pinocchio’s arm.
He attempted to leave, but the boys closed around him laughing.
They called him the teacher’s darling and a painted wooden saint.
Under the steady pressure of insult, Pinocchio’s patience began splitting.
“Return my things now, and this can finish,” he warned.
The tallest boy answered by kicking a book towards the incoming water.
Pinocchio caught it just before the next wave reached its pages.
When he rose, anger had pushed every sensible intention from his mind.
The boys opened their own books and transformed lessons into weapons.
Grammar, arithmetic, and history sailed across the beach in dangerous curves.
Pinocchio ducked beneath a dictionary, then hurled an atlas in reply.
Nobody remembered that books should expand minds rather than bruise bodies.
Loose pages scattered over the shore like pale, frightened birds.
During the confusion, a heavy textbook struck a boy named Eugene.
He collapsed without moving as a dark mark appeared above his eye.
The noisy beach became suddenly and completely silent.
Pinocchio released the book in his hands and knelt beside Eugene.
“Help me carry him somewhere safe,” he begged the others.
Fearing blame, every classmate fled towards the shelter of the dunes.
Pinocchio remained, even though loyalty made him look responsible.
He pressed a handkerchief against the injury and shouted desperately for help.
Two police officers heard his calls and hurried down the beach.
They discovered one wooden puppet beside a wounded boy and scattered books.
Pinocchio described the entire fight and identified every classmate who had fled.
Unfortunately, one of his books rested nearest Eugene’s motionless hand.
The officers assumed that this convenient evidence explained the injury.
They praised Pinocchio for remaining there, then arrested him nevertheless.
“Justice would improve if you waited for truth,” Pinocchio protested.
As they marched towards town, the officers noticed his astonishing nose.
One feared that such a remarkable prisoner would attract an unmanageable crowd.
A sudden sea wind lifted Pinocchio’s cap and carried it down the shore.
“Allow me to retrieve it,” he asked, and one officer relaxed his grip.
Pinocchio recovered the cap, then raced beside the breaking waves.
The officers released their powerful mastiff, Alidoro, to hunt him down.
Alidoro’s long legs rapidly consumed the distance between hunter and fugitive.
Pinocchio reached the water and continued straight into the sea.
His wooden body floated naturally, allowing him to swim beyond the breakers.
The heavy dog followed, obeying training where judgement should have intervened.
Soon, Alidoro’s paws weakened and his dark head slipped beneath the surface.
He called to Pinocchio, promising freedom in exchange for rescue.
Pinocchio could simply have continued towards safety beyond the dog’s reach.
Instead, Geppetto’s disappearing boat returned sharply to his memory.
He turned, caught Alidoro’s collar, and began pulling towards the beach.
The current resisted them, but Pinocchio would not release the exhausted animal.
At last, one generous wave rolled them together onto the sand.
Alidoro coughed, found his breath, and reconsidered the prisoner before him.
“A life rescued creates a debt,” he said, limping away.
Pinocchio entered the water again before the officers could return.
A powerful current swept him beyond the headland and hidden from view.
There, an enormous fishing net suddenly climbed beneath his wooden feet.
Pinocchio landed among silver fish, armoured crabs, and twisting eels.
The net belonged to a Green Fisherman whose hair resembled wet seaweed.
After careful inspection, he classified Pinocchio as an unusually wooden fish.
“Whatever species you are, frying should improve you,” he concluded.
He dragged the heavy net into a smoky cave above the shore.
One by one, he rolled the fish through flour beside an enormous pan.
Pinocchio explained repeatedly that puppets belonged neither in nets nor dinners.
The Fisherman laughed, then coated him from wooden nose to heel.
Flour turned Pinocchio white while hot oil hissed over the fire.
The Fisherman lifted him by one leg and moved towards the pan.
At that instant, fierce barking echoed against the cave walls.
Alidoro had followed Pinocchio’s familiar scent around the rocky headland.
The dog leapt between the astonished Fisherman and his intended meal.
While the man protected his dinner, Alidoro seized the flour-covered puppet.
He carried Pinocchio outside and bounded across the slippery rocks.
Pinocchio brushed white powder from his face and thanked the dog warmly.
“Today, each of us pulled the other from danger,” Alidoro replied.
He also brought news that Eugene was recovering from the blow.
The fleeing boys had confessed, so the officers no longer accused Pinocchio.
Relief warmed him, although shame about joining the fight remained.
He was innocent of the gravest injury, not innocent of every mistake.
Alidoro left him near the road, and Pinocchio continued home alone.
Night had settled when he reached the Fairy’s tall, unwelcoming door.
He knocked softly, then firmly, and finally with both fists.
After a remarkable delay, a Snail answered from a distant upstairs window.
She promised to descend, although snails and puppets understand urgency differently.
Hours passed while Pinocchio paced, pleaded, complained, and shivered outside.
Eventually, anger defeated endurance, and he kicked the heavy wooden door.
His foot broke through one panel and became firmly trapped.
Now Pinocchio could neither enter the house nor escape his ridiculous position.
The Snail arrived at sunrise and discovered him exhausted against the door.
She presented a breakfast whose appearance promised comfort from a distance.
The bread was plaster, the chicken cardboard, and the shining fruit stone.
Hunger turned the visual joke into one more painful lesson.
Pinocchio fainted from exhaustion and later awoke safely inside the house.
The Fairy listened without interruption as he reconstructed the entire day.
Pinocchio confessed the absence, the fight, the escape, and the impatient kick.
He refused to hide behind either his classmates’ lies or the officers’ mistake.
The Fairy repaired his wooden foot while leaving responsibility firmly in place.
Throughout the following months, Pinocchio returned faithfully to his lessons.
He studied, helped at home, and chose friends who respected his effort.
When insults came, he remembered how quickly anger had once multiplied.
His teacher watched steady discipline replace bright but temporary enthusiasm.
At the year’s conclusion, Pinocchio achieved the highest place in his class.
The Fairy announced that tomorrow he would finally become a real boy.
She prepared a celebration and invited him to gather his companions.
Pinocchio thanked her, already imagining the joy on Geppetto’s tired face.
Before the party, he insisted upon inviting each friend personally.
He hurried into town searching for Lamp-Wick, the school’s laziest pupil.
Humanity appeared almost within reach, while temptation waited patiently after dark.
