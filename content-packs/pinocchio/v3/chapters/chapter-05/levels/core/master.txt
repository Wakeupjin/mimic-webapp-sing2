At dawn, the blue-haired Fairy sent a Falcon to the oak.
Its sharp beak cut the rope, and Pinocchio dropped gently below.
A great Dog carried the puppet to the white house.
The Fairy placed him in a warm bed beside a bright fire.
Though wooden, Pinocchio seemed weakened by sorrow.
She summoned three doctors to examine the unusual patient.
An Owl listened wisely, a Raven considered everything, and a Cricket watched.
The Owl declared that Pinocchio might be alive or possibly not.
The Raven agreed that the opposite might also be true.
The Cricket offered a simpler diagnosis: the patient was disobedient.
Pinocchio heard every word and began crying beneath the blanket.
His tears proved enough life for the doctors to end their debate.
The Fairy thanked them and prepared a bitter medicine.
Pinocchio sat up, saw the dark spoonful, and refused immediately.
The Fairy offered sugar first, but he wanted sweetness without medicine.
She explained that unpleasant help could still be necessary help.
Pinocchio folded his lips and refused to drink it.
Then four solemn Rabbits entered carrying a small wooden box.
They announced that they had come for a patient who rejected treatment.
Pinocchio grabbed the cup and swallowed every drop in one brave gulp.
The Rabbits left disappointed, while the Fairy hid a smile.
Within minutes, warmth returned to Pinocchio’s arms and legs.
“The medicine was not as terrible as I imagined,” he admitted.
“Fear often tells a larger story than truth,” the Fairy replied.
Safe in bed, Pinocchio moved four coins from his mouth to his pocket.
The Fairy asked how he had come to the oak alone.
Pinocchio began honestly, describing Fire Eater and the five coins.
When he reached the Fox and Cat, his courage weakened.
“I lost every coin somewhere on the road,” he claimed.
At once, his nose stretched across the pillow.
Startled, Pinocchio added that strangers had taken the money from him.
The nose grew beyond the bed and pushed against the wall.
Finally, he insisted that he had never owned any gold at all.
Now the nose trapped him beneath wooden branches.
The Fairy laughed kindly, but she did not rescue him immediately.
“A lie becomes visible even when nobody can see your nose,” she said.
Pinocchio confessed that four coins were still safely hidden in his pocket.
He also admitted ignoring warnings because the miracle sounded easy.
The Fairy called Woodpeckers to shorten the troublesome nose.
Their tapping filled the room until Pinocchio’s face looked normal again.
He promised to tell the truth before another branch could grow.
The Fairy said Geppetto was coming and might arrive by morning.
Joy lifted Pinocchio from the bed before he had fully recovered.
He begged to meet his father on the road and guide him home.
The Fairy agreed, but warned him to take the direct path.
“A straight road still requires a steady choice,” she reminded him.
Pinocchio thanked her and hurried through the forest.
He rehearsed an honest confession for Geppetto with every step.
Near the edge of town, two familiar figures waited beside a fountain.
The Fox leaned on one leg, and the Cat wore a new bandage.
They greeted Pinocchio as if nothing unusual had happened.
The Fox claimed assassins had delayed their journey.
The Cat nodded sadly and hid the paw that Pinocchio had bitten.
Pinocchio accused them of leaving him with the bill.
They praised his generosity and blamed the confusion on urgent business.
Then the Fox asked whether he still wished to help poor Geppetto.
The Field of Wonders, he said, was nearby.
Pinocchio remembered the Fairy’s warning and began to walk past them.
But the Fox described a tree heavy with enough gold for a lifetime.
The picture of Geppetto in comfort weakened Pinocchio’s new resolve.
He told himself that noble intentions could excuse one final shortcut.
Soon, the three travellers entered a dusty place called Simpleton Town.
Pinocchio saw injured animals begging beside richly dressed thieves.
Workers looked hungry, while tricksters rode in carriages.
The strange town should have warned him, but hope closed his eyes.
At the Field of Wonders, the Fox selected a patch of dry earth.
Pinocchio dug a hole and placed the four coins inside.
He covered them carefully and poured water over the loose soil.
The Fox instructed him to fetch more water from a distant stream.
By the time he returned, both companions had vanished.
Pinocchio waited, watching the silent patch for a golden shoot.
One hour passed, then another, but the earth remained empty.
A Parrot nearby began laughing at him.
“Why are you laughing when my fortune is growing?” Pinocchio asked.
The Parrot explained that fools imagined money could appear without effort.
While Pinocchio fetched water, the Fox and Cat had stolen every coin.
He dug furiously and found only four small holes in the soil.
The truth hurt more because several honest voices had predicted it.
Pinocchio ran to the courthouse and demanded justice.
A stern Gorilla judge listened while guards recorded the complaint.
Pinocchio named the thieves, described their trick, and showed the empty hole.
The judge nodded gravely and announced a surprising decision.
“This puppet has been foolish enough to lose his own money,” he said.
“Therefore, prison will give him time to become wiser.”
Pinocchio protested that victims should not be punished for reporting crimes.
The guards agreed privately, but they still followed the judge’s order.
An iron door closed behind Pinocchio in a cold stone cell.
Across the corridor, cheerful criminals laughed at the town’s idea of justice.
Pinocchio finally had nothing left to hide, sell, plant, or promise.
He thought of the Fairy waiting and Geppetto searching the road.
Twice he had ignored the truth because a sweeter story pleased him.
This time, the result could not be blamed on hunger or darkness.
He had recognised the trick and chosen it again.
Days passed, and Pinocchio listened to the prisoners’ dishonest boasts.
Their pride showed him what repeated excuses could eventually create.
At last, a royal celebration brought an order releasing every criminal.
The jailer refused Pinocchio because he claimed to be innocent.
Thinking quickly, Pinocchio declared himself the greatest rascal of all.
The confused jailer unlocked the door and sent him away.
Freedom tasted strange when it arrived through yet another false statement.
Pinocchio stepped into daylight, determined to find Geppetto and the Fairy.
He had survived the Field of Wonders, but not mastered its lesson.
Ahead waited a long road where clever words would not be enough.
