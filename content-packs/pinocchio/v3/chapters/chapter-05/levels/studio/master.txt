As dawn silvered the forest, the blue-haired Fairy dispatched a Falcon to the oak.
One stroke of its beak severed the rope, lowering Pinocchio from the nightmare above.
A magnificent Dog gathered the limp puppet and carried him towards the white house.
There, the Fairy settled him beside a fire that glowed like a second sunrise.
Although his body was wood, grief had drained its usual mischief and motion.
Concerned by this peculiar silence, she summoned three celebrated doctors.
An Owl arrived with grave wisdom, a Raven with questions, and a Cricket with honesty.
After lengthy thought, the Owl announced that Pinocchio might possibly be alive.
The Raven solemnly agreed that the exact opposite remained equally possible.
The Cricket ignored their grand language and diagnosed a badly disobedient child.
Beneath the blankets, Pinocchio heard the judgement and began to sob.
Those indignant tears proved more useful than every word of medical debate.
The Fairy dismissed the doctors, then prepared a spoonful of bitter medicine.
Pinocchio sat upright, examined its dark surface, and refused with impressive speed.
She offered him sugar first, but sweetness alone was the bargain he preferred.
The Fairy explained that necessary help rarely asks permission to taste pleasant.
Pinocchio tightened his mouth, determined to outlast both medicine and reason.
Then four sombre Rabbits entered, carrying a little wooden box between them.
They announced that they collected patients who chose danger over treatment.
Pinocchio seized the cup and swallowed everything before the Rabbits reached his bed.
The disappointed procession withdrew while the Fairy carefully concealed her amusement.
Within moments, warmth travelled back through Pinocchio’s wooden arms and legs.
“The medicine was smaller than the monster in my imagination,” he confessed.
“Fear enjoys improving a story,” replied the Fairy, “but truth usually makes it manageable.”
In bed, Pinocchio moved four coins from his mouth to his pocket.
The Fairy asked how a boy carrying gold had ended alone beneath an oak.
He began truthfully, describing Fire Eater, the theatre, and the generous five coins.
Once the Fox and Cat entered the tale, however, his brave voice weakened.
“Somewhere on the road, every coin simply disappeared,” he claimed.
His nose immediately lengthened across the pillow like a growing branch.
Startled by the evidence, Pinocchio changed course and blamed unknown travellers.
The nose stretched beyond the bed until its tip pressed against the wall.
Desperate now, he insisted that no gold had ever belonged to him.
Branches burst from the enormous nose and pinned their owner beneath them.
The Fairy’s laughter remained kind, but her rescue did not arrive immediately.
“Most lies become visible,” she said, “even without such helpful carpentry.”
At last, Pinocchio confessed that four coins remained safely hidden in his pocket.
He admitted choosing the miracle because patience had sounded less exciting.
The Fairy summoned Woodpeckers, whose rapid beaks reduced the wooden evidence.
Their tapping filled the room until Pinocchio’s familiar face returned.
He promised to choose truth before his nose needed pruning again.
The Fairy then revealed that Geppetto was approaching and might arrive tomorrow.
Joy pulled Pinocchio from bed before strength had fully returned to him.
He begged permission to meet his father and escort him through the forest.
The Fairy agreed, provided he followed the direct road without another adventure.
“Even a straight path demands steady feet,” she warned him gently.
Pinocchio thanked her and hurried beneath the shadowed branches.
With every step, he rehearsed the honest confession Geppetto deserved to hear.
Near Simpleton Town, two familiar silhouettes waited beside a cracked fountain.
The Fox balanced dramatically on one leg, while the Cat displayed a fresh bandage.
They welcomed Pinocchio with the smooth affection of friends who owed nothing.
According to the Fox, dangerous assassins alone had prevented their earlier return.
The Cat sighed supportively while hiding the paw Pinocchio had bitten.
Pinocchio reminded them that their disappearance had left him with the bill.
They renamed his payment generosity and blamed everything else on urgent business.
Then the Fox asked whether Pinocchio still wished to rescue poor Geppetto from poverty.
The famous Field of Wonders, he whispered, lay almost within sight.
Pinocchio remembered the Fairy’s instruction and managed several steps past them.
The Fox then painted a golden tree, heavy with enough coins for a lifetime.
In Pinocchio’s mind, Geppetto exchanged hunger and cold for comfort and peace.
That loving picture quietly weakened the promise Pinocchio had just rehearsed.
He decided that noble intentions could surely excuse one final shortcut.
Entering Simpleton Town beneath dust, they saw injured animals beg as elegant thieves passed in carriages.
Honest workers looked thin, yet successful tricksters wore silk and jewels.
The entire town offered a warning, but Pinocchio preferred the Fox’s brighter story.
At the Field of Wonders, the Fox selected an especially lifeless patch of soil.
Pinocchio buried his four coins with the care of a hopeful gardener.
He pressed down the earth and poured water over his invisible fortune.
The Fox sent him towards a distant stream for one final jug.
When Pinocchio returned, both advisers had disappeared with remarkable efficiency.
He watched the silent ground, waiting for the first golden leaf to emerge.
An hour became two, while the dry soil produced nothing but dust.
From a nearby branch, a Parrot began laughing with merciless delight.
“Why laugh,” asked Pinocchio, “when my fortune is preparing to grow?”
The Parrot explained that money rewarded work, not wishes buried in dirt.
While Pinocchio fetched water, the Fox and Cat had recovered every coin.
He clawed through the soil and uncovered only four neat, empty holes.
The discovery hurt because several honest voices had already described this ending.
Furious and ashamed, Pinocchio rushed to the courthouse demanding justice.
A stern Gorilla judge received his complaint while sleepy guards recorded it.
Pinocchio named the thieves, reconstructed the trick, and presented the empty field.
The judge considered these facts before announcing an astonishing conclusion.
“This puppet possessed enough foolishness to lose his own property,” he declared.
“A period in prison should therefore improve the quality of his judgement.”
Pinocchio argued that punishing a victim merely rewarded the people who cheated him.
The guards privately agreed, but public orders still carried the keys.
An iron door closed, leaving Pinocchio alone inside a cold stone cell.
Across the corridor, experienced criminals applauded Simpleton Town’s remarkable justice.
Pinocchio had nothing remaining to hide, invest, exaggerate, or promise.
He pictured the Fairy waiting at home and Geppetto searching an empty road.
Twice, a pleasing story had defeated a truth he already understood.
This failure could not be excused by hunger, darkness, or surprise.
He had recognised the trap clearly and entered it of his own choice.
Days passed as Pinocchio listened to prisoners celebrate their dishonest achievements.
Their pride revealed what a lifetime of repeated excuses might finally produce.
Then a royal festival brought orders to release every criminal in the prison.
The jailer excluded Pinocchio because the puppet continued claiming innocence.
Thinking quickly, Pinocchio announced that he was the greatest rascal among them.
Defeated by this logic, the jailer opened the door and sent him outside.
Freedom felt strangely bitter because another false statement had purchased it.
Pinocchio stepped into daylight, determined to find both Geppetto and the Fairy.
He had escaped the Field of Wonders without escaping the habit behind it.
Ahead stretched a road where clever language could no longer replace responsible action.
