CHAPTER 01 — A Voice in the Wood

Long ago, Master Cherry worked in a small Tuscan town.
He was an old carpenter with a round, red nose.
One cold morning, he found a plain block of wood.
The block stood beside his busy workbench.
It looked strong enough for a table leg.
Master Cherry picked up his axe and smiled.
Before he hit the wood, a voice cried, “Please stop!”
The carpenter froze with the axe above his head.
He looked under the bench and behind the door.
He opened every cupboard, but nobody was there.
“I heard nothing,” he said, although his legs shook.
He slowly raised the axe for a second try.
The axe hit, and the voice shouted, “That hurt me!”
Master Cherry dropped the axe and stared at the block.
Wood could not talk, but this block clearly could.
He tried to make its surface smooth with a plane.
The tool touched the wood, and the block began laughing.
Master Cherry fell backwards into a basket of shavings.
His face turned white, red, and then white again.
“Someone is playing a trick,” he whispered.
He touched the block with one careful finger.
The block stayed quiet.
Then he pressed it a little harder.
A long, unhappy sound came from inside the wood.
Just then, someone knocked loudly on the shop door.
Master Cherry nearly jumped out of his apron.
He pushed the block away and called, “Come in!”
Geppetto entered in an old coat with many patches.
He had little money, but he had big ideas.
“I have a wonderful plan,” he told his friend.
“I will make a wooden boy who can dance.”
“He will fence and turn through the air too.”
The puppet could perform and earn money for food.
But Geppetto also wanted someone beside him at home.
His one small room felt lonely every night.
“I only need a good piece of wood,” he said.
A soft laugh came from the dark corner.
Master Cherry looked at the strange block again.
He decided that Geppetto should take it.
“You can use that block,” he said quickly.
Geppetto bent down and reached for the wood.
Before he touched it, a voice called, “Hello, Polendina!”
That rude name made fun of Geppetto’s yellow wig.
Geppetto turned around with an angry face.
“Did you say that?”
Master Cherry said he had not spoken.
The two old friends did not believe each other.
They shouted, waved their arms, and stamped their feet.
Soon they rolled across the floor in a silly fight.
At last, both men stopped because they were tired.
Geppetto fixed Master Cherry’s collar.
Master Cherry brushed dust from Geppetto’s coat.
“Are we friends again?” Geppetto asked.
“Yes, friends again,” Master Cherry answered.
The block gave one small, happy laugh.
Master Cherry wrapped it and gave it to Geppetto.
On the way home, Geppetto imagined a happy crowd.
He saw the puppet dancing while people clapped.
His real home was only one cold room.
A fire painted on the wall gave no heat.
Geppetto put the block on his workbench.
Then he lit a small lamp.
“What name shall I give you?” he wondered.
After a moment, he smiled.
“Your name will be Pinocchio.”
The name sounded bright and full of fun.
Geppetto made his tools sharp and ready.
“Let us make a boy and a family,” he said.
First, he carved the hair, head, and two eyes.
The new eyes looked straight at him.
“Please look kindly at your father,” Geppetto said.
The eyes turned up towards the ceiling.
Next, Geppetto made a long nose.
He cut it shorter, but it grew again.
Then he carefully shaped a mouth.
The mouth smiled and started laughing at him.
“You have no body, but you are already rude.”
The smile became even wider.
Geppetto carved the neck, body, arms, and hands.
The new hands quickly took his yellow wig.
Pinocchio waved the wig and laughed loudly.
“Give it back,” Geppetto said with a sad voice.
He finished the wooden legs and feet.
Then Geppetto placed Pinocchio on the floor.
Pinocchio kicked his nose and pulled away.
One step became two, and then he started running.
He knocked over a stool and jumped across the bed.
“Stop!” Geppetto cried.
Pinocchio opened the door and ran outside.
He raced past shops, chickens, and laughing children.
Geppetto followed in slippers and called his name.
Pinocchio did not turn around.
Being free felt exciting, and danger felt far away.
A police officer stood in the middle of the road.
He caught Pinocchio by his very long nose.
The puppet’s wooden legs kept running in the air.
Geppetto arrived tired but happy to see him.
“Thank you for stopping my boy,” he said.
“I will take him safely home now.”
Geppetto reached for ears that he had never carved.
He held Pinocchio’s neck, so the puppet fell down.
People thought Geppetto was hurting the wooden boy.
The officer listened to the crowd, not the facts.
He let Pinocchio go and took Geppetto away.
Pinocchio ran free while his father went to prison.
“I made you with love, but not enough sense.”
Pinocchio’s first hard lesson was now very close.

CHAPTER 02 — Hunger, Home, and Sacrifice

Pinocchio hurried home and hoped to see Geppetto there.
But the little room was dark, cold, and empty.
He called for his father, but nobody answered.
Then he heard a slow sound near the fire.
A Talking Cricket came out of the shadows.
“Bad choices often bring trouble,” the Cricket said.
Pinocchio crossed his arms and called advice boring.
He wanted to eat, sleep, and go anywhere.
The Cricket said that careless freedom could bring hunger.
“I will find someone who gives me dinner,” Pinocchio answered.
“People work or study for their food,” said the Cricket.
Pinocchio stamped his foot and told him to leave.
But the Cricket stayed and continued speaking honestly.
Pinocchio became angry before he stopped to think.
He picked up a wooden hammer and threw it.
The hammer hit the wall near the Cricket.
After one small sound, the Cricket disappeared.
The room became quiet, and Pinocchio felt alone.
He said that he did not need anyone.
Then his empty stomach made a very loud sound.
He searched Geppetto’s cupboard and found only dust.
He looked inside every jar, basket, and pan.
There was nothing to eat anywhere.
Pinocchio blamed Geppetto and the whole world.
He did not blame his own bad choices.
Then he found one white egg under some wood.
To the hungry puppet, it looked like a feast.
He put a pan beside the painted fire.
Then he remembered that the fire was not real.
He found a small heater and made a flame.
“Now I can eat,” he said, opening the egg.
But a small yellow chick jumped out.
“Thank you for opening my door,” it said.
The chick’s mother had hidden the egg there.
It flew through the window and over the roofs.
Pinocchio looked at the empty shell in surprise.
His dinner had escaped before reaching the pan.
The Cricket’s words now sounded more helpful.
Still, Pinocchio did not want to say so.
He decided that someone in town would feed him.
Outside, cold rain fell on the dark streets.
Pinocchio knocked on a door and asked for bread.
A tired man looked down from an upstairs window.
“Wait there,” he said, holding a large bowl.
Pinocchio hoped that the bowl held hot soup.
Instead, cold water fell all over his head.
He ran away while the window closed.
At another house, a large dog barked at him.
The rain became heavier, and the streets felt dangerous.
At midnight, Pinocchio stopped feeling brave.
He came home wet, tired, sorry, and hungry.
The Cricket was gone, so nobody heard his apology.
Pinocchio put his wet feet near the small fire.
He planned to rest for only one minute.
Soon, he was fast asleep.
The hot coals slowly burned his wooden feet away.
Morning found him beside two piles of ash.
Then Geppetto knocked loudly at the door.
Pinocchio jumped up and fell onto the floor.
He looked down and saw that his feet were gone.
“Please open the door,” Geppetto called.
“I cannot because someone ate my feet!” Pinocchio cried.
Geppetto thought that his son was playing a game.
Pinocchio tried to move, but he rolled backwards.
At last, Geppetto climbed through the window.
He saw Pinocchio on the floor and forgot his anger.
Geppetto picked him up before asking any questions.
Pinocchio quickly told him about the whole night.
He spoke about the Cricket, egg, rain, and fire.
He also gave many reasons for his own mistakes.
Geppetto listened until Pinocchio finished.
“Hunger taught you an important lesson,” he said.
Then Geppetto took three pears from his pocket.
The pears were going to be his breakfast.
Pinocchio asked him to remove the skins and cores.
He ate the soft fruit and wanted more.
Geppetto put the skins and cores on the table.
Pinocchio complained, but then he saw his tired father.
Geppetto had come from prison with very little food.
Pinocchio ate every skin and core.
“Simple food can taste very good,” he said.
Geppetto smiled because Pinocchio was finally thankful.
Then Pinocchio looked down and cried about his feet.
He promised to study, work, listen, and help Geppetto.
Geppetto knew that a promise was only a beginning.
However, he chose to trust his son again.
He carefully made two new feet from wood.
Pinocchio waited and tried not to move.
Soon, Geppetto fixed both feet in place.
Pinocchio danced, but he stopped when Geppetto asked.
He hugged his father and promised to attend school.
The next morning, Geppetto made simple clothes for him.
The jacket was paper, and the shoes were bark.
His funny cap came from a piece of bread.
Pinocchio felt as fine as a prince.
Then he remembered that pupils needed a schoolbook.
Geppetto had no money to buy one.
“Wait here,” he said, putting on his winter coat.
He went outside while Pinocchio practised writing.
Later, Geppetto returned with a new alphabet book.
He had no coat, and his thin shirt shook.
“Where is your coat?” Pinocchio asked quietly.
“It was too warm,” Geppetto answered with a weak laugh.
Pinocchio held the important book against his chest.
He understood that Geppetto had given up something precious.
At sunrise, Pinocchio started walking towards school.
Far ahead, exciting music waited beside the road.

CHAPTER 03 — The Puppet Theatre

Pinocchio left home with his new alphabet book.
He planned to learn and help Geppetto later.
First he would read, write, and learn numbers.
One day, he could buy Geppetto another coat.
These good plans went with him towards school.
For a while, Pinocchio walked like a serious pupil.
Then he heard drums and pipes across the town.
His feet slowed, but he told them to continue.
“School stays all day, but music can leave,” he thought.
He would listen for one minute and return.
The music led him into a busy square.
Bright flags moved above a travelling puppet theatre.
The alphabet book suddenly felt heavy under his arm.
A large sign promised battles, heroes, and funny surprises.
Pinocchio pushed through the crowd to the ticket window.
A ticket cost four pennies, but he had none.
He offered his paper coat and bark shoes.
He even offered his strange cap made of bread.
The ticket seller did not want any of them.
Pinocchio looked at the book from Geppetto’s coat.
For a moment, he remembered his cold father.
Then the drums became louder, and he forgot.
Another boy bought the book from Pinocchio.
Pinocchio used the money for a theatre ticket.
He went inside before he could feel sorry.
Children sat in front of a small stage.
Pinocchio found a seat as the lights became low.
The curtain opened on Harlequin and Punchinello.
The two puppets argued in a painted garden.
The children laughed until Harlequin suddenly stopped.
He looked into the crowd and saw Pinocchio.
“Look, friends!” he cried. “A brother is here!”
Every puppet on the stage turned around.
They left the play and ran to Pinocchio.
Together, they lifted him above the happy crowd.
Pinocchio had never received such a warm welcome.
The puppets called him brother, cousin, and friend.
They danced while the musicians played faster.
The children clapped, although the play had stopped.
For a few minutes, Pinocchio forgot all his problems.
Then a heavy boot hit the floor backstage.
The music stopped, and every puppet became still.
The theatre belonged to a huge man called Fire Eater.
His black beard reached all the way down.
His eyes were red, and his voice was very loud.
“Who stopped my show?” he asked angrily.
The puppets pointed at Pinocchio with shaking hands.
Fire Eater picked him up and carried him backstage.
Pinocchio stopped feeling brave at once.
A large fire was cooking Fire Eater’s dinner.
But the weak flames could not finish the food.
Fire Eater looked at Pinocchio like dry firewood.
“A wooden puppet will burn well,” he said.
Pinocchio shook until his wooden joints made noise.
He called for Geppetto and spoke about the lost coat.
Fire Eater listened, but his face stayed hard.
Then he gave three very large sneezes.
The other puppets said that sneezing showed kindness.
Pinocchio saw some hope and asked again.
Fire Eater still needed wood for his dinner.
He decided to save Pinocchio and burn Harlequin.
Two helpers pulled Harlequin towards the fire.
Harlequin said goodbye and tried to smile.
The other puppets covered their faces sadly.
Pinocchio saw that his friend was now in danger.
His happy feeling quickly changed to shame.
He stood between Harlequin and the fire.
“Please burn me instead,” Pinocchio said.
“I will not let my friend die for me.”
Fire Eater looked at Pinocchio in surprise.
He told the helpers to put Harlequin beside him.
“Do you understand your choice?” he asked.
Pinocchio felt afraid, but he nodded.
He remembered how Geppetto gave up his warm coat.
Maybe courage also meant giving up something important.
The fire made small sounds behind him.
Everyone in the theatre waited quietly.
Fire Eater’s nose moved, and he sneezed again.
“I will not burn either puppet,” he shouted.
The cooks had to finish dinner without them.
Harlequin hugged Pinocchio with great joy.
All the puppets cheered and danced around them.
Fire Eater looked happy, although he tried not to.
Another sneeze stopped his order for silence.
That night, the puppets shared a large meal.
They ate bread and fruit behind the stage.
Pinocchio told them about Geppetto’s poor home.
He explained the painted fire and the sold coat.
Fire Eater’s angry face became a little softer.
He asked where Geppetto lived and worked.
Pinocchio answered him honestly this time.
Telling the truth felt new but good.
Fire Eater left and went into his wagon.
Harlequin thanked Pinocchio for saving him.
“You acted like a true brother,” he said.
Those words made Pinocchio feel very proud.
Then he remembered the missing alphabet book.
He told Harlequin that he had sold it.
Harlequin said good choices could follow bad ones.
One mistake did not control every later choice.
Before morning, the puppets danced for Pinocchio.
Fire Eater watched quietly from the dark.
After the dance, he called Pinocchio forwards.
“You made trouble, but you were brave,” he said.
The big man reached under his long beard.
He pulled out a heavy bag of coins.
Pinocchio heard the bright sound of gold.
Morning and the road home waited outside.
This time, he wanted to bring Geppetto real help.

CHAPTER 04 — Five Coins and a Dangerous Road

Fire Eater opened his bag and counted five gold coins.
“Take this money to your father,” he told Pinocchio.
The coins could buy food, wood, books, and a coat.
Pinocchio thanked him, and Fire Eater sneezed.
At sunrise, Pinocchio left the theatre for home.
He held one hand over the coins.
He imagined Geppetto’s happy and surprised face.
Each step made five small promises ring.
He would buy another book and fill the cupboard.
Nothing would stop him on the way home.
Then two travellers spoke to him in the square.
One was a Fox, and one was a Cat.
The Fox walked badly, and the Cat acted blind.
They smiled in a friendly but strange way.
The Fox asked why Pinocchio looked so happy.
Pinocchio proudly showed them his five gold coins.
The Cat suddenly saw the gold very clearly.
The Fox told him about the Field of Wonders.
In that field, a planted coin became a tree.
By morning, the tree would grow many gold coins.
Pinocchio imagined a warm new home for Geppetto.
The Fox sounded helpful and wise.
“Why work slowly when gold can grow?” he asked.
A Blackbird called to Pinocchio from a branch.
“Do not believe easy miracles from strangers,” it warned.
The Cat jumped up, and the bird flew away.
The Fox said that some birds trusted nobody.
Pinocchio felt worried, but he wanted the gold.
He agreed to walk with his new friends.
They said the field was almost on his way.
At night, they reached the Red Lobster Inn.
The Fox said good investors needed a large dinner.
He ordered meat, fish, bread, and sweet food.
The Cat ate a lot while still acting blind.
Pinocchio ate only nuts to save money.
The Fox and Cat praised his careful choice.
Then they all went to sleep until midnight.
Pinocchio put the coins safely under his pillow.
He dreamed about a tree full of gold.
At midnight, the innkeeper woke him.
The Fox and Cat had already left.
They had told Pinocchio to pay for dinner.
Pinocchio gave the innkeeper one gold coin.
Now their friendly story felt less true.
The innkeeper told him to reach the field alone.
Outside, the road was dark, narrow, and quiet.
The four coins made sounds in Pinocchio’s pocket.
Bare trees reached over the road like fingers.
The Talking Cricket sat on a low stone.
He looked pale, like a memory or ghost.
“Turn back and take the honest road,” he said.
Pinocchio said his friends would make him rich.
The Cricket said good friends paid their own bills.
For a moment, Pinocchio almost went home.
Then he felt ashamed about being tricked.
He walked forwards and ignored the Cricket.
Two dark shapes moved behind him.
They wore bags over their heads.
“Give us your money,” one shape ordered.
Pinocchio knew the Fox’s walk and Cat’s voice.
He quickly put the four coins into his mouth.
The thieves caught him and pulled at his jaw.
Pinocchio closed his teeth and kicked hard.
One thief held his arms tightly.
The other thief reached near his mouth.
Pinocchio bit the paw, and the Cat cried out.
He pulled free and ran into the forest.
The two thieves followed close behind.
Pinocchio crossed water and jumped over roots.
The coins hit together behind his teeth.
Then he saw a small white house.
A child with blue hair stood at a window.
Pinocchio asked her to open the door quickly.
She said nobody alive was inside the house.
The window closed, and Pinocchio was alone.
He knocked again, but nobody opened the door.
The two thieves came into the garden.
They asked for the coins one more time.
Pinocchio could not speak with a full mouth.
They searched his clothes and wooden body.
But the thieves did not find the gold.
They decided to frighten him.
They tied Pinocchio to a long rope.
Then they lifted him into a large oak tree.
Strong wind and rain moved the branches.
The thieves waited for Pinocchio to give up.
But he kept the coins inside his mouth.
Soon the thieves became cold and tired.
They promised to return with tools at sunrise.
Then they left Pinocchio alone in the storm.
The rope held him while the wind turned him.
He stopped thinking about the golden tree.
He thought about Geppetto waiting at home.
He remembered the coat and the lost book.
Every easy choice had taken him farther away.
“Father,” he tried to call through the coins.
Lightning showed the white house again.
A small blue light appeared behind one window.
Someone inside had finally seen him.
Pinocchio became weak as the storm continued.
His hands opened, and his head fell forwards.
The coins now felt empty and useless.
Then he heard large wings above the tree.
A great bird flew around the oak.
It called loudly towards the white house.
A door opened as the blue light grew.
At last, help was coming towards Pinocchio.
But could it reach him before sunrise?

CHAPTER 05 — The Blue Fairy and the Field of Wonders

Early that morning, the Blue Fairy sent a Falcon.
The bird cut the rope around Pinocchio’s neck.
A large Dog carried him to the white house.
The Fairy put Pinocchio beside a warm fire.
His wooden body looked weak and very still.
She called three doctors to examine him.
An Owl, a Raven, and a Cricket arrived.
The Owl said Pinocchio might be alive.
The Raven said he might be dead.
The Cricket said, “He is a disobedient boy.”
Pinocchio heard this and cried under the blanket.
His tears showed that he was truly alive.
The Fairy thanked the doctors and made some medicine.
Pinocchio saw the dark medicine and shook his head.
The Fairy offered sugar, but he still refused.
“Bad tastes can still help you,” she explained.
Pinocchio closed his mouth as tightly as possible.
Then four Rabbits entered with a small box.
They came for a patient who refused help.
Pinocchio quickly drank all the bitter medicine.
The Rabbits left, and the Fairy smiled softly.
Soon, warmth returned to his wooden arms and legs.
“It was not as bad as I feared,” he said.
“Fear often makes small things seem large,” she replied.
Pinocchio moved four coins from his mouth into his pocket.
The Fairy asked why he was alone near the oak.
Pinocchio first told her about Fire Eater.
Then he reached the part about the Fox and Cat.
“I lost all my money,” he said nervously.
At once, his nose grew across the pillow.
He changed the story and blamed some strangers.
His nose reached past the bed towards the wall.
Then he said he never had any coins.
Now wooden branches trapped him under his long nose.
The Fairy laughed gently but waited before helping.
“People can notice lies without seeing this nose,” she said.
Pinocchio admitted that four coins were safe in his pocket.
He also admitted that he wanted easy money.
The Fairy called Woodpeckers to shorten his nose.
Their beaks tapped until his face looked normal.
Pinocchio promised to tell the truth next time.
The Fairy said Geppetto was coming to the house.
Pinocchio jumped from the bed with sudden joy.
He wanted to meet his father on the road.
The Fairy let him go by the direct path.
“A straight road still needs a good choice,” she said.
Pinocchio thanked her and entered the forest.
He practised telling Geppetto the whole honest story.
Near town, he saw two figures by a fountain.
The Fox stood on one leg beside the Cat.
They greeted Pinocchio like old and trusted friends.
The Fox said dangerous men had delayed them.
The Cat hid the paw that Pinocchio had bitten.
Pinocchio asked why they left him with the bill.
They praised him and blamed some urgent business.
Then the Fox spoke about helping poor Geppetto.
The Field of Wonders was very close, he said.
Pinocchio remembered the Fairy and tried to leave.
The Fox described a tree full of gold coins.
Pinocchio imagined Geppetto living in a warm home.
That picture made his new promise feel weaker.
He chose one last shortcut for a good reason.
In Simpleton Town, hurt animals begged while rich thieves rode past.
Hungry workers watched dishonest people live well.
The town looked wrong, but Pinocchio ignored the warning.
The Fox chose a dry place in the field.
Pinocchio dug a hole and buried four coins.
He covered them and poured water on the soil.
The Fox sent him to a far stream.
When Pinocchio returned, the Fox and Cat were gone.
He waited for a golden tree to grow.
One hour passed, but the ground stayed empty.
A Parrot in a nearby tree laughed loudly.
“Why are you laughing at my fortune?” Pinocchio asked.
“Money does not grow without work,” said the Parrot.
The Fox and Cat had stolen the coins.
Pinocchio dug and found only four empty holes.
The truth hurt because others had already warned him.
Pinocchio ran to court and asked for justice.
A Gorilla judge listened to his full complaint.
Pinocchio named the thieves and explained their trick.
The judge became serious and gave his decision.
“You were foolish and lost your own money.”
“So prison will give you time to think.”
Pinocchio said a victim should not receive punishment.
The guards agreed, but they followed the order.
They locked Pinocchio inside a cold stone cell.
Real criminals laughed at this strange kind of justice.
Pinocchio now had no money, tricks, or excuses.
He thought about the Fairy and his worried father.
Twice, he had chosen a sweet story over truth.
This choice did not happen because of hunger.
He had known the trick and followed it again.
Several days passed inside the dark prison.
The criminals proudly told stories about their bad choices.
A royal celebration finally freed every criminal.
The jailer kept Pinocchio because he said he was innocent.
Pinocchio quickly called himself a terrible criminal too.
The confused jailer opened the door and freed him.
Freedom came because Pinocchio told another false thing.
He walked into daylight and searched for his family.
The lost coins had taught him only part of the lesson.
The next road would test his actions, not his words.

CHAPTER 06 — The Snake, the Trap, and the Dog

The prison gate opened, and Pinocchio hurried towards the Fairy.
He felt sorry for all his broken promises.
This time, he wanted to tell no excuses.
The road crossed a quiet field in warm sunlight.
A huge green Serpent blocked the whole road.
Smoke came from its nose, and its eyes burned.
Pinocchio stopped, and his wooden legs shook.
“Please move your tail,” he asked the Serpent.
The creature did not answer or move.
Pinocchio tried to climb over its long body.
The tail rose, and Pinocchio fell into the mud.
The Serpent laughed at the muddy wooden boy.
It laughed until it fell down, so Pinocchio escaped.
Pinocchio cleaned his face and continued walking.
He had eaten nothing, and every smell made him hungry.
Purple grapes hung behind a low vineyard wall.
“I will take one bunch,” Pinocchio told himself.
He climbed over the wall and touched the grapes.
An iron trap closed around both wooden ankles.
Pinocchio pulled hard, but the trap stayed closed.
Each movement made the metal teeth hold tighter.
Cold rain fell across the dark vineyard.
Pinocchio remembered all the warnings he had ignored.
“I found a reason to steal,” he whispered sadly.
Only the small crickets heard his honest words.
A farmer finally arrived with a bright lantern.
The farmer used the trap to stop chicken thieves.
He saw Pinocchio and the damaged grape plants.
“A wooden thief is still a thief,” he said.
Pinocchio admitted that hunger had led him there.
The truth could not remove his wrong choice.
The farmer decided not to send him to prison.
Instead, he took Pinocchio to the chicken coop.
An empty kennel stood beside a heavy chain.
The farmer’s old watchdog, Melampo, had recently died.
“You will guard my chickens tonight,” said the farmer.
He locked a collar around Pinocchio’s wooden neck.
Pinocchio sat by the kennel and felt deep shame.
Now freedom seemed less important than being honest.
At midnight, four thin Weasels entered the farmyard.
They expected to see Melampo beside the coop.
Their leader spoke to Pinocchio like a business partner.
The Weasels often stole eight chickens each week.
They gave Melampo one chicken for staying quiet.
“Take the same deal,” she whispered to Pinocchio.
Pinocchio remembered the grapes and his heavy collar.
He did not want to make another dishonest choice.
However, he nodded and acted like he agreed.
The Weasels entered the coop to take the chickens.
Pinocchio shut the door and dropped the bar.
He barked until the farmer turned on a light.
The farmer came outside and found the trapped thieves.
He asked Pinocchio how he knew their secret.
Pinocchio could have blamed the dead watchdog.
Instead, he told the farmer about the full offer.
He admitted that he had tricked the Weasels.
The farmer listened and removed the hard collar.
“You stole grapes, but you saved my chickens.”
Pinocchio promised that he would never steal food again.
“Keep that promise when hunger returns,” said the farmer.
At sunrise, the farmer opened the farm gate.
Pinocchio received no prize, but he felt lighter.
Honesty changed his next step, not his past mistake.
At noon, Pinocchio reached the Fairy’s old home.
The bright house and garden were completely gone.
A small white stone stood in the wild grass.
The words said that the Blue Fairy had died.
They said Pinocchio’s desertion had broken her heart.
He fell down and held the cold stone.
“Please return, and I will be better,” he cried.
The empty field gave him no answer.
Pinocchio cried until his voice became very weak.
He remembered the Fairy’s care and patient help.
Every lost second chance now felt very precious.
The sky grew dark above the silent stone.
Pinocchio stayed with the pain he had caused.
Then he heard strong wings moving above him.
A large Pigeon landed beside the white stone.
The Pigeon knew Geppetto and knew Pinocchio too.
“Your father has searched everywhere,” the bird said.
Geppetto had walked through many towns and forests.
Then he built a little boat near the sea.
He wanted to search across the ocean for Pinocchio.
Pinocchio stood up with both hope and fear.
The sea was more than a thousand miles away.
“Climb on my back,” the kind Pigeon said.
They flew high above houses, roads, and fields.
They crossed cold clouds and rain all night.
In the morning, Pinocchio smelled the salty sea.
The Pigeon landed near a worried crowd.
A tiny boat rose and fell on huge waves.
Pinocchio saw Geppetto holding the boat’s oars.
“Father!” he shouted into the loud storm.
Geppetto saw Pinocchio and waved his old cap.
Then a dark wave crashed against the boat.
For a moment, Geppetto and the boat disappeared.
People begged Pinocchio to stay on the beach.
He remembered how far Geppetto had searched.
Pinocchio dropped his cap and entered the sea.
His wooden body floated, but the storm was strong.
He swam past the waves and called for Geppetto.
Night came, and the beach disappeared behind him.
Still, he swam towards his father’s lost boat.
Pinocchio did not know what waited ahead.
He only knew he would not leave Geppetto again.

CHAPTER 07 — The Sea and the Land of Busy Bees

Pinocchio swam until the storm became weaker.
At sunrise, the sea carried him towards an island.
He reached the rocks, cold, tired, and alone.
He could not see Geppetto or the small boat.
Pinocchio called, and a Dolphin came near.
“Did you see my father?” Pinocchio asked.
The Dolphin had seen a boat far away.
The Terrible Shark was also swimming near it.
That sea creature could swallow a whole ship.
Pinocchio felt great fear inside his wooden chest.
The Dolphin did not know if Geppetto escaped.
It showed Pinocchio a road across the island.
Pinocchio thanked the Dolphin and started walking.
The road entered a busy town full of workers.
Bakers carried bread, and shops opened their doors.
Builders worked while children carried small boxes.
Fresh bread made Pinocchio’s empty stomach growl.
He asked a charcoal seller for one coin.
The man offered coins for pushing his heavy cart.
Pinocchio refused because the black dust looked dirty.
Next, he asked a mason for some food.
The mason offered bread for carrying a basket.
Again, Pinocchio refused because the work looked hard.
Other people offered jobs, but nobody offered free food.
The town expected every person to help.
Soon, Pinocchio’s pride felt heavier than any basket.
At a fountain, he met a woman with water jugs.
She gave him fresh water to drink.
Then Pinocchio smelled food inside her basket.
“Carry one jug, and you can eat,” she said.
Her house stood at the top of a hill.
Pinocchio thought about the heavy water jug.
His hungry stomach gave him the honest answer.
He lifted the jug and followed her home.
His feet slipped, and his shoulders began to hurt.
However, he carried every drop to the house.
The woman gave him bread, vegetables, and something sweet.
The simple meal tasted wonderful after his work.
Pinocchio learnt that honest work could feel good.
While eating, Pinocchio looked carefully at the woman.
Her eyes looked familiar, and her hair was blue.
“You look like an old friend,” he said.
The woman asked him to name that friend.
Pinocchio spoke about the little Blue Fairy.
“I am that Fairy, but I grew older,” she said.
Time had moved on during Pinocchio’s wild adventures.
He hugged her and called her his little mother.
The Fairy loved him but remembered his past choices.
The white memorial stone still made Pinocchio shake.
His sadness and his wish to change were real.
For the first time, he felt safe again.
He asked if Geppetto could still be alive.
“Hope for him, and become a good son,” she said.
Pinocchio wanted to become a real boy at once.
The Fairy said a real boy needed good habits.
He must learn, work, speak truth, and care.
Pinocchio promised to practise these things every day.
“A promise begins the work,” the Fairy told him.
Next morning, she gave Pinocchio books for school.
He imagined Geppetto seeing him as a student.
The first letters and numbers seemed difficult.
When Pinocchio wanted to leave, he remembered the collar.
He remembered the water jug and earned meal too.
Those memories helped him stay in his seat.
Soon, learning became more interesting than he expected.
Some pupils laughed at his wooden head.
At first, Pinocchio answered with angry words.
Later, he learnt to give a calm reply.
He studied each night and finished his lessons early.
The teacher praised his hard and steady work.
Many classmates liked him, but some felt jealous.
Those boys wanted fun without waiting or working.
They laughed when Pinocchio chose his homework.
Every good choice made another choice easier.
One morning, the class had an important lesson.
Some boys whispered outside before the school bell.
They said the Shark was near the beach.
Pinocchio quickly thought about his missing father.
“It may know where Geppetto is,” he said.
The boys promised they could see the monster.
They asked him to miss only one lesson.
Pinocchio looked towards his waiting classroom.
Then he looked down the road to the sea.
He remembered the Fairy’s words about actions.
But he feared that Geppetto was inside the Shark.
“I will look once and return,” Pinocchio decided.
The boys smiled, but Pinocchio did not notice.
They ran away, and he followed them.
The road crossed fields and went downhill.
The sound of the sea became louder.
Pinocchio said that finding Geppetto was more important.
He ignored the boys when they laughed.
Soon, he could not see the school roof.
The empty beach appeared in front of them.
No dark fin moved through the quiet water.
Only white birds flew above the bright sea.
Pinocchio looked around, and his hope grew cold.
“Where is the Shark?” he asked angrily.
The boys stopped laughing but offered no honest answer.
Their jealous faces showed why they had brought him there.
Pinocchio looked back towards the far town.
One small choice had carried him very far.
Missing class was no longer his biggest problem.
The boys spread around him, mocking his books and promises.
At last, their leader stepped forward to explain the trick.

CHAPTER 08 — The Beach and a Better Year

The boys admitted that the Shark story was completely false.
They used the lie to pull Pinocchio from school.
His good work made the lazy boys feel bad.
“Go back to class,” Pinocchio said calmly.
One boy grabbed his cap and threw it.
Another boy knocked his schoolbooks onto the sand.
Pinocchio tried to leave, but they blocked him.
They called him silly names and laughed loudly.
Pinocchio’s careful patience started to disappear.
“Give back my things,” he warned them.
The tallest boy kicked a book towards the water.
Pinocchio caught it before the wave arrived.
He stood up, and anger filled his mind.
The boys opened their books like heavy weapons.
Books about words and numbers flew across the beach.
Pinocchio avoided one book and threw another back.
They forgot that books should help people learn.
Loose pages moved across the sand in the wind.
A heavy book suddenly hit a boy named Eugene.
He fell down, and his head was hurt.
Everyone became quiet at once.
Pinocchio dropped his book and ran to Eugene.
“Help me carry him,” Pinocchio begged.
The frightened boys ran away from the beach.
Pinocchio stayed, although people might blame him.
He covered Eugene’s injury and called for help.
Two police officers heard the loud call.
They found Pinocchio beside the injured boy.
Pinocchio explained the fight and named the others.
One of his books was lying near Eugene.
The officers believed that book had hit him.
They thanked Pinocchio for staying but arrested him.
“Please wait for the truth,” Pinocchio said.
The officers noticed his very long wooden nose.
They feared it would bring a large crowd.
A sudden sea wind tore Pinocchio’s cap from his head.
He asked to retrieve it, and the officers loosened their grip.
He grabbed the cap and raced away.
The officers sent their large dog, Alidoro.
Alidoro quickly came closer to Pinocchio.
Pinocchio reached the sea and entered the waves.
His wooden body floated well in deep water.
The heavy dog followed without thinking carefully.
Soon, Alidoro became tired and started sinking.
He promised not to catch Pinocchio after a rescue.
Pinocchio could have continued swimming away.
Instead, he remembered Geppetto’s boat in the storm.
He turned, held Alidoro, and pulled him.
The sea pushed back, but Pinocchio kept trying.
A strong wave finally carried them onto the beach.
Alidoro breathed again and looked at Pinocchio.
“You saved me, and I will remember,” he said.
Pinocchio returned to the sea before the officers came.
A strong current carried him behind some high rocks.
Then a large fishing net rose under him.
Pinocchio fell among fish, crabs, and eels.
A strange Green Fisherman owned the net.
He looked at Pinocchio like a rare fish.
“You will taste good fried,” said the Fisherman.
He carried the full net into a dark cave.
He covered each fish with white flour.
Pinocchio explained that he was a wooden puppet.
The Fisherman laughed and covered him with flour.
White dust reached from Pinocchio’s head to feet.
Hot oil moved loudly inside a large pan.
Suddenly, angry barking filled the cave.
Alidoro had followed Pinocchio’s smell to the rocks.
The dog jumped between Pinocchio and the pan.
The Fisherman turned, and Alidoro grabbed Pinocchio.
Alidoro carried him outside and ran away.
Pinocchio cleaned his face and thanked the dog.
“We saved each other today,” Alidoro replied.
He said Eugene was getting better now.
The other boys confessed, so Pinocchio was free.
Pinocchio felt relief, but the fight still shamed him.
He had not caused the injury, but he had still fought.
Alidoro left, and Pinocchio walked home alone.
It was night when he reached the Fairy’s door.
He knocked softly, loudly, and then very hard.
A Snail answered from a high window.
She said she would come down slowly.
Pinocchio waited outside for many cold hours.
At last, he kicked the door in anger.
His wooden foot broke through and became stuck.
He could not enter or walk away.
The Snail reached him when the sun rose.
She brought a breakfast that looked very good.
The bread was plaster, and the fruit was stone.
Pinocchio understood the painful joke while feeling hungry.
He fainted and later awoke inside the house.
The Fairy listened to his complete story.
Pinocchio admitted missing school, fighting, and kicking.
He did not blame only the other boys.
The Fairy repaired his foot, but not his responsibility.
For many months, Pinocchio went to school daily.
He studied, helped the Fairy, and chose kind friends.
When people teased him, he controlled his anger.
His teacher saw his slow and steady change.
At year’s end, Pinocchio was the best pupil.
The Fairy promised he would become a real boy.
She planned a party for all his friends.
Pinocchio imagined Geppetto smiling at the wonderful news.
He wanted to invite every friend himself.
He searched town for the lazy boy Lamp-Wick.
His dream was close, but new trouble waited.

CHAPTER 09 — The Land of Toys

The Fairy’s bright house was busy with party work.
Tomorrow, Pinocchio would become a real boy.
Small cakes, fruit, and sweets waited on a long table.
Twelve chairs stood ready around it for his school friends.
Pinocchio felt proud of his recent hard work at school.
He ran through town to invite everyone before evening.
However, he could not find his friend Lamp-Wick.
Lamp-Wick was thin, restless, and very lazy.
Pinocchio searched streets and playgrounds until the sky became dark.
At last, he found Lamp-Wick hiding beneath a farm porch.
“Please come to my party tomorrow,” Pinocchio said.
Lamp-Wick looked towards the empty road with a secret smile.
He was waiting for a special midnight wagon that would leave town.
The wagon was going to the Land of Toys.
Lamp-Wick said that nobody studied or worked there.
There were no schools, books, teachers, or lessons.
Children played all day and every day.
“I will live there forever,” Lamp-Wick announced.
Pinocchio remembered the Fairy and her promise.
Still, a life of easy games sounded wonderful.
He said that he only came to invite Lamp-Wick.
Lamp-Wick asked him to wait for the wagon.
One minute became ten under the bright moon above the quiet fields.
Pinocchio promised to leave before the wagon.
Lamp-Wick kept describing the amazing place.
By midnight, Pinocchio was still beside him.
Soon they heard small bells in the distance.
A large wagon appeared through the darkness with yellow lanterns.
It was completely full of children squeezed tightly together.
Twelve tired donkeys with wet eyes pulled the heavy wagon.
Lamp-Wick climbed inside with great excitement.
There was no open seat for Pinocchio.
The driver offered him a donkey’s back.
Pinocchio remembered his party and stopped.
Then the donkey spoke softly through its bridle in a tired voice.
“Turn back before you become like me.”
The driver hit the animal and laughed as if nothing had happened.
Pinocchio saw fear inside the donkey’s eyes.
However, he climbed on for a short visit.
The wagon left the Fairy’s empty chairs behind.
At sunrise the next morning, they entered the Land of Toys.
Bells, drums, and happy voices came from every house.
Some children raced through crowded squares while others flew large kites.
Theatres showed funny plays all day without stopping.
Sweet shops with cakes and sweets never closed their colourful doors.
Nobody carried a bag or went to work.
Pinocchio and Lamp-Wick tried every game.
They built towers, chased parades, and broke toys until they fell asleep.
The next day was exactly the same, without clocks or chores.
Soon the days became one long holiday.
Sometimes Pinocchio remembered the waiting Fairy.
He played louder because the memory hurt.
Five months passed without school or useful work.
One morning, Pinocchio felt an itch above his head.
He touched two long and hairy ears.
At first, he thought Lamp-Wick was joking.
Then the mirror showed his own donkey ears.
Pinocchio hid them under a very large cap.
He locked the door and searched for answers.
The ears continued growing during the day.
Soon the cap could not cover them.
Pinocchio became too frightened to stay alone.
He called for help from a doctor.
An old Marmot came into the room.
She knew many illnesses of careless children.
She checked his ears, hands, and wooden feet.
Then her face became serious and sad.
“You have Donkey Fever,” she told him.
Children without duties slowly lost their childhood.
Soon Pinocchio would become a full donkey.
He asked for medicine or another chance.
The Marmot could only shake her head.
This change had started many months before.
Every ignored warning had made it stronger.
Pinocchio remembered the donkey under the wagon.
Now he knew that it had been a child.
After the Marmot left, Pinocchio ran outside.
He hurried towards Lamp-Wick’s small house.
Lamp-Wick opened the door with a huge cap.
Both boys tried not to look closely.
Pinocchio asked Lamp-Wick to remove his cap.
Lamp-Wick told Pinocchio to go first.
They counted to three and uncovered together.
Two pairs of donkey ears stood straight up.
For a moment, both friends stayed silent.
Then nervous fear became wild laughter.
Their laughter changed into loud donkey brays.
Hands became hooves, and faces became longer.
Grey hair covered their changing bodies.
Long tails grew behind them at last.
Soon two frightened donkeys lay on the floor.
Outside, the wagon driver heard the braying.
He entered with ropes and locked the bedroom door.
Toyland had always hidden his secret business.
It turned careless children into animals he could sell.
Pinocchio tried to say his own name.
Only another sad donkey sound came out.
Lamp-Wick kicked, but the driver tied both boys.
He kept them side by side beneath his watch.
Pinocchio finally understood every warning on the midnight road.
The tired donkey under the wagon had once been a child.
He remembered the Fairy’s twelve empty chairs.
A promised holiday had become a terrible trap.
Another wagon of laughing children arrived outside.
Before dawn, the driver announced that the morning market waited.
Neither frightened friend could escape what morning would bring.

CHAPTER 10 — Donkey Days

Pinocchio woke the next morning as a grey donkey.
Lamp-Wick stood beside him with his ears down.
Both boys felt afraid and deeply ashamed.
The Little Man kicked open the bedroom door.
He smiled as if nothing bad had happened.
Then he brushed their coats until they looked bright.
The boys tried to speak, but only brayed.
The Little Man led them to a busy market.
Selling careless boys was his secret business.
A farmer bought Lamp-Wick for work on his farm.
A circus owner came to look at Pinocchio.
He checked the donkey’s legs, teeth, and eyes.
Then he bought Pinocchio and pulled him away.
The circus stable was cold and smelled of hay.
Pinocchio did not want to eat the dry food.
His new master became angry and punished him.
Every day, Pinocchio had to learn new tricks.
He bowed, danced, and counted to the music.
He walked on two legs around the ring.
He also jumped through hoops and over boards.
Bright posters soon covered walls across the town.
They promised a donkey who danced like a man.
One evening, many families filled the circus tent.
Pinocchio entered with ribbons and a small hat.
The crowd cheered, but he felt trapped inside.
The music started, and Pinocchio gave a deep bow.
He marched in circles and balanced a glass.
Then he looked towards a seat near the front.
A beautiful lady wore his picture around her neck.
Soft blue hair showed under her silver veil.
Pinocchio knew that she was the Blue Fairy.
Sudden hope made him forget his next trick.
“Fairy, I am here!” he tried to shout.
His mouth made only a loud donkey sound.
The crowd laughed because they thought it was funny.
The Fairy looked sad and left her seat.
Pinocchio kept watching the empty place.
The circus owner shouted for the final jump.
Pinocchio ran towards a bright paper hoop.
His front feet hit it, and he fell.
A sharp pain moved through his left leg.
The music stopped, and the people became quiet.
The owner only cared about the broken show.
An animal doctor checked Pinocchio the next morning.
“This donkey will always walk badly,” he said.
The owner decided to sell Pinocchio at once.
Most buyers did not want an injured donkey.
At last, a man who made drums stopped.
He looked closely at Pinocchio’s thick skin.
The man wanted the skin for a drum.
He bought Pinocchio for only four small coins.
The man took him to a cliff by the sea.
He tied a heavy stone around Pinocchio’s neck.
He tied another rope around one back leg.
Pinocchio pulled away, but his leg was weak.
The man talked happily about his new drum.
Then he pushed the frightened donkey from the cliff.
Pinocchio fell through the air into cold water.
The stone pulled him towards the sea floor.
The man waited above and held the rope.
Pinocchio sank into dark and silent water.
He thought about Geppetto and his lost home.
Suddenly, many little fish swam around him.
They began eating the grey donkey covering.
They did not bite the wooden body inside.
Soon the long ears and tail were gone.
Pinocchio’s wooden arms and legs appeared again.
He moved his fingers and kicked both feet.
The Fairy’s magic had protected him again.
The man pulled up the rope at last.
A living wooden puppet came out of the sea.
The man opened his mouth in great surprise.
“Where is my donkey?” he asked loudly.
“I am that donkey,” Pinocchio answered with a laugh.
He quickly told the man what had happened.
The angry man tried to catch him again.
Pinocchio removed the rope and jumped away.
His wooden body could swim very well.
He left the cliff far behind him.
A white rock stood ahead in the sunlight.
A small blue Goat waited on the rock.
She called to him in the Fairy’s voice.
“Swim faster, Pinocchio!” the Goat cried.
Then the water behind him made a terrible sound.
Small fish rushed away from a huge shadow.
The Terrible Shark opened its cave-like mouth.
Water pulled Pinocchio towards its great teeth.
The blue Goat called again from the rock.
Pinocchio fought hard against the strong water.
The Shark swallowed him in one great rush.
Pinocchio fell into cold water and darkness.
He heard the Shark breathing all around him.
Pinocchio called out, and someone answered.
A large Tunny was floating close to him.
The Tunny had lost all hope of escape.
He told Pinocchio to accept the dark place.
Pinocchio would not give up so easily.
Then he saw a tiny golden light far away.
No sunlight could enter the Shark’s body.
“Someone may be there,” Pinocchio told the Tunny.
He thanked the fish and walked towards the light.
The path was wet, cold, and completely dark.
Still, the little light grew brighter with every step.
Pinocchio was no longer running towards easy fun.
This time, he was trying to find his way home.

CHAPTER 11 — Inside the Shark

Pinocchio left the Tunny and followed the small light through heavy darkness.
Cold, greasy water covered the floor under his wooden feet.
The Shark’s breathing sounded like faraway thunder.
The golden light seemed like a star inside a black sky.
Pinocchio kept walking because waiting in one place could not help him.
Soon he saw a candle burning inside a glass bottle.
It stood in a bottle on a little table.
Boxes and wet barrels from a broken ship lay all around.
An old man sat beside the candle and ate a small fish.
His white hair and thin face looked familiar.
Pinocchio moved closer through the dirty water and held his breath.
The old man slowly looked up at him.
Pinocchio knew that the man was Geppetto.
His wooden knees shook with sudden joy, and he almost fell.
“Father, I have found you!” he cried.
Geppetto rubbed his eyes in great surprise.
Pinocchio threw his arms around Geppetto’s neck.
They laughed and cried together in the weak light.
For a moment, neither one could speak clearly.
Geppetto touched the puppet’s face as if checking a wonderful dream.
Pinocchio promised not to leave him again.
“Are you really my Pinocchio?” Geppetto asked.
“Yes, and I searched everywhere for you.”
The Shark moved, and the candle shook above the small table.
Still, their dark corner now felt like home.
Then Geppetto finally asked about Pinocchio’s long and difficult journey.
Pinocchio began with the book bought for school.
He admitted selling it for the puppet show.
He told Geppetto about Fire-Eater and five coins.
Then he described the clever Fox and Cat.
He spoke about the false Field of Wonders.
He also confessed his lies and growing nose.
The Blue Fairy had saved him many times.
However, Pinocchio had broken many promises.
He told Geppetto about prison and the watchdog job.
He spoke about school fights and the sea.
On Busy Bee Island, he had started working.
Then Lamp-Wick had invited him to Toyland.
Pinocchio had chosen play and become a donkey.
“I chose fun before love,” he said sadly.
Geppetto held his son close and forgave him after the story.

A strong storm broke his little boat.
The Terrible Shark swallowed him with the pieces.
At first, Geppetto thought he would die there.
Later, the Shark swallowed a ship with food.

He had counted time by each burning candle.
Now only a little food was left.
The candle on the table was also the last.
“We must escape before it dies,” said Pinocchio.
Geppetto looked down at his weak legs.
“I cannot walk far or swim,” he answered.
Pinocchio did not let fear grow between them.
“You cared for me, and I will carry you.”
Geppetto stared at him with proud surprise.
His careless puppet had become very different.
Pinocchio looked for a safe way outside.
Geppetto knew that the old Shark had asthma.
It slept each night with its mouth open.
That open mouth could be their way out.
The candle was short, so they moved quickly.
Geppetto held the bottle and Pinocchio’s hand.
Together they climbed through the Shark’s throat.
The walls moved whenever the monster breathed.
They walked slowly and made almost no sound.
At last, cool air touched Pinocchio’s face.
Moonlight came through the Shark’s great mouth.
Its tongue looked like a very wide road.
Three rows of teeth stood before the sea.
Geppetto looked down and held Pinocchio tightly.
“Stay near me,” Pinocchio whispered to him.
They moved towards the edge of the tongue.
Suddenly, the Shark took a deep breath.
Its huge body became tight around them.
Then the Shark gave a powerful sneeze.
The wind threw father and son backwards.
They fell down the throat into the stomach.
The candle hit the water and went out.
“Now we are lost,” Geppetto said quietly.
Pinocchio was afraid, but he stayed calm.
“We are together, and I remember the way.”
He found Geppetto’s hand in the darkness.
They climbed the long throat a second time.
Pinocchio waited for the Shark’s slow breathing.
Then they crossed the tongue very carefully.
They passed all three rows of teeth.
At the edge, Geppetto saw the dark sea.
Pinocchio bent down in front of him.
Geppetto climbed onto the puppet’s strong back.
“Hold on tightly, Father,” Pinocchio told him.
He jumped past the teeth into cold water.
Geppetto’s weight pulled him down at first.
Then Pinocchio’s wooden body rose again.
He began swimming under the bright moon.
The Shark stayed asleep far behind them.
No land could be seen across the water.
Pinocchio used the stars to choose a direction.
Geppetto rested quietly against his back.
Every stroke moved them farther from the Shark.
The sea was dangerous, but they were together.

CHAPTER 12 — A Real Boy

Pinocchio swam through the night with Geppetto on his back.
He said that land was not far away.
However, he could not see it clearly.
Geppetto shook from cold, fear, and hunger.
Soon Pinocchio’s wooden arms became very weak.
“Help me, Father,” he whispered in the water.
A friendly voice answered from somewhere nearby.
The Tunny had followed them out of the Shark.
He lifted both travellers onto his broad back.
Then he carried them safely towards the shore.
At sunrise, Pinocchio helped Geppetto onto the sand.
He kissed the kind Tunny and thanked him.
Father and son walked away to find a home.
Two poor animals were begging beside the road.
They were the Fox and Cat from long ago.
The Cat was blind, and the Fox had no tail.
Their lies had left them hungry and alone.
They called Pinocchio their friend and asked for money.
Their voices sounded kind, but he remembered everything.
They had stolen his coins with false promises.
“You tricked me once, but not again,” he said.
The Fox gave excuses, and the Cat copied him.
Pinocchio did not shout or laugh at them.
He took Geppetto’s arm and walked away.
Now he could see danger inside sweet words.
He no longer wanted an easy road to riches.
Soon they saw a tiny cottage made of straw.
A hidden voice told them to open the door.
They entered, but the little room looked empty.
Then they saw the Talking Cricket above them.
The Cricket remembered Pinocchio’s terrible hammer throw.
Pinocchio did not try to defend himself.
He lowered his head and said he was sorry.
“Please help my father, even if you punish me.”
The Cricket believed this honest and simple apology.
He welcomed them into the cottage to stay.
The Blue Fairy had given him the little house.
Pinocchio cried because she thought he was dead.
Then he made a soft straw bed for Geppetto.
Geppetto needed milk, but they had no money.
The Cricket sent Pinocchio to Farmer John.

The rough rope hurt his hands badly.
Each full bucket felt heavier than the last.
Pinocchio wanted to stop many times.
Then he remembered his weak father at home.
He finally poured the last bucket into the trough.
Farmer John gave him warm and sweet milk.
Pinocchio carried every drop home to Geppetto.
The next morning, he did the work again.
Daily work slowly made his father stronger.
At the farm, Pinocchio saw a sick donkey.
The animal lay quietly on some dirty straw.
Its tired eyes seemed very familiar to him.
Pinocchio bent down and asked its name.
“I am Lamp-Wick,” the donkey answered weakly.
Pinocchio knew his old friend at once.
Toyland had led Lamp-Wick to this hard life.
The poor donkey died before help could come.
The farmer only saw an animal without value.
Pinocchio saw a friend and a painful warning.
He returned home and told Geppetto the sad news.
The memory helped him make better choices.
Pinocchio returned to the well every morning.
He continued this hard work for five months.
He also learnt to make baskets from reeds.
He sold the baskets and bought food.
Then he built a rolling chair for Geppetto.
They went outside together on warm, bright days.
Every night, Pinocchio studied beside a lamp.
He bought an old book with missing pages.
He made ink from dark berries.
He wrote words with a sharp wooden stick.
Learning was slow, but Pinocchio kept trying.
His father grew healthy, and their home felt peaceful.
After paying their costs, Pinocchio saved fifty pennies.
He wanted a new coat, hat, and shoes.
This time, he had truly earned the reward.
On his way to market, he met the Snail.
She used to live with the Blue Fairy.
The Snail said that the Fairy was very ill.
She had no money to buy food.
Pinocchio gave the Snail all fifty pennies.
“My old clothes can wait,” he said.
He promised to work harder and send more.
At home, Geppetto asked about the new clothes.
Pinocchio said that he would shop another day.
That night, he made sixteen baskets instead of eight.
He worked quietly until the clock reached midnight.
Then he slept beside his dear father.
The Blue Fairy came to him in a dream.
She looked healthy, warm, and very happy.
She praised his loving and helpful choices.
“You helped others when it was difficult,” she said.
The Fairy forgave his old mistakes and kissed him.
Morning sunlight filled a beautiful new room.
Pinocchio looked down at two warm hands.
His wooden body had become a living boy.
He saw bright eyes and brown hair in the mirror.
New clothes and fifty gold coins waited nearby.
Healthy Geppetto was working in the next room.
Pinocchio ran to him with happy tears.
Geppetto said Pinocchio’s choices had changed their home.
The old wooden puppet rested beside a chair.
Pinocchio looked at it without feeling ashamed.
Magic had changed his body during one night.
Love and hard work had changed his heart first.
At last, the real boy and his father were home.
